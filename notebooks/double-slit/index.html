Double Slit: Three Accounts — Notebook

Electrons sent one at a time through two slits build up bands on a screen. All accounts agree on that pattern and on how to calculate it. They disagree about what happens between the source and the screen. The demonstrations are the N-Slits app, embedded and set up for each part: press a set-up button, then Start.

Reading path: Parts 1–4 introduce the experiment; Part 5 compares three views; Part 6 solves the full Schrödinger equation, with reflection at the wall and pilot-wave trajectories. Use Brief for the demonstrations or Full for explanations and mathematics.

Parts 1–5 use N-Slits at the Basic level with its analytical engine, a simplified model without wall reflections. Part 6 uses its numerical engine. Each embedded app keeps its own settings; Open in N-Slits ↗ opens the same set-up in the full app.

Before you begin: reading the pictures

Every demonstration shows the experiment from above. The electrons come in from the left, the wall with the slits is in the middle, the detector screen is on the right. The moving coloured pattern is the wavefunction ψ: red and blue are its high and low points (its phase), and the brighter the colour, the stronger the wave. The brightness is enhanced so that weak parts stay visible; it is not an absolute probability scale. The stripes are the wave, not extra particles and not the bands counted on the screen. The white panel on the right of the screen counts the hits at each height y (bars), and the green curve gives the probability of a hit there.

1. Two alternatives

ψ = ψU + ψL

With both slits open, the state is a sum of a part from the upper slit and a part from the lower slit. These are two terms describing one electron, not two electrons.

2. Add, then square

P(y) ∝ |ψ(y)|²

The probability of a hit is the squared size of the total ψ at that point. Because the parts are added first, they can reinforce or cancel.

3. One run, one dot

one electron → one hit

A single electron makes one spot at one place. The bands appear only after many electrons, each sent alone.

1. The puzzle: two slits are not one slit plus one slit ≈ 8 min

With one slit open, electrons spread into one broad hump on the screen. Open both and the screen shows bands, including places that each slit alone would reach but that are now nearly empty.

What to notice: Compare Add the two humps with Both slits. Opening the second slit makes some places on the screen less likely, not more.\[\Delta y \approx \frac{\lambda Z}{d}\]

Start with Upper slit and Lower slit. Then press Add the two humps: this is the prediction when the two alternatives contribute independent probabilities. (N-Slits shows it by putting a which-slit detector on each slit; Part 4 explains why that gives exactly the sum of the two humps.) A definite path alone does not imply this rule: in Pilot Wave, a particle uses one slit while its guiding wave uses both. Then press Both slits.

The spacing of the bright bands depends on the wavelength λ and the slit separation d. For a screen at distance Z behind the wall (the full app calls it Screen dist. (D−L)), it is close to λZ/d. Lowering λ squeezes the bands together, and so does moving the slits apart. Bands appear only where the two humps overlap, and the humps decide how many of them are bright: move the slits far apart and the humps separate, reducing the contrast of the interference. The panel below the controls shows the measured spacing, and says when the humps barely overlap.

Predict first: Suppose the two slit alternatives contribute independent probabilities. What does that model predict?

Each button starts a new run with that set-up. Change λ and the slit separation in the panel: does Δy ≈ λZ/d hold?

Details and math: where λ comes from

De Broglie's relation λ = h/p gives any particle with momentum p a wavelength. The default λ = 50 nm is a very slow electron. All lengths are in nanometers so that the source, slits and screen fit in one picture; real electron experiments use other scales, with the same structure of bands. Bach and coworkers (2013) did the full experiment with electrons, opening and closing each slit with a movable mask. λ stops at 70 nm in N-Slits, to stay in the range where the model is accurate.

Δy ≈ λZ/d holds when the screen is far compared with the slit separation. Each slit alone gives a broad hump (the single-slit envelope); the bands are drawn under it, so only a few are bright.

After comparing the curves: What needs explaining when both slits are open?

2. One electron, one hit ≈ 6 min

The smooth curve is never seen directly. The wavefunction extends through both slits, while the screen records one spot at one place. What the wavefunction says about the electron differs between the accounts in Part 5.

What to notice: One electron per wave sends a single electron in each packet. Its wave splits at the slits and spreads, yet when it reaches the screen exactly one hit appears. After many electrons, the hits follow the curve.\[P(y)\propto|\psi(y)|^2\]

Where a single dot lands cannot be predicted; only the frequencies can. The rule that connects the wave to the dots is the Born rule: the probability of a hit near y is proportional to |ψ(y)|². Each electron is sent alone, so the bands are not produced by electrons interacting with one another. This was first done with electrons by Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi (1976) and, in a famous version, by Tonomura and coworkers (1989). Both used an electron biprism, a charged wire that splits the beam in two, which plays the role of the two slits.

Predict first: What will the screen show after one electron?

Each wave in the picture stands for that many electrons, sent one at a time; each makes one hit.

3. Add, then square ≈ 8 min

Think of each part of the wave at a point P as an arrow, like the hand of a clock. Its length says how strong that part is; its direction says where in its cycle the wave is when it arrives. To get the probability, add the two arrows tip to tail first, then take the length of the result and square it.

What to notice: Drag P (or move the slider) to a dark band. The two arrows have about the same length and point in opposite directions, so they cancel. Near the center, the two geometric distances differ by about half a wavelength.\[|\psi_U+\psi_L|^2=|\psi_U|^2+|\psi_L|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi_U^{*}\psi_L)\]

The straight colored lines measure distances from the slits to P; they are not particle trajectories. The arrows represent wave amplitudes, not directions of motion.

In the language of complex numbers, the arrows are ψU and ψL. In the formula above, the first two terms are the two humps of Part 1. The last, the interference term, can be positive or negative; it makes the bands.

The colored lines in the figure run from each slit to P. Their lengths rU and rL differ, and every extra wavelength of path turns an arrow once around. Near the center, where the two arrows have about the same length, bright bands sit where rL − rU is a whole number of wavelengths and dark bands halfway between. Farther out, one slit's arrow is much shorter than the other, so the arrows can no longer cancel completely and the bands wash out. That is the single-slit envelope of Part 1 seen with arrows.

Try it: When two equal-length arrows point in opposite directions, what happens to the probability at P?

Details and math: the arrows

The arrows are ψU(P) and ψL(P), rotated together so that ψU points right; only their relative angle matters for |ψ|². Their lengths are scaled so that the peak of one slit alone has length 1. The relative angle is close to 2π(rL − rU)/λ, with small corrections from the curvature of the beams.

4. Which slit? A record at one slit ≈ 8 min

Place a detector at the upper slit that records whether the electron passed there. The record can be any physical change: a click, a flipped spin, a moved atom. Nobody needs to look at it. N-Slits has an ideal detector: its record always tells the two paths apart. The small figure after it shows a partial record.

What to notice: As the record becomes reliable, the bands fade, behind the wall and on the screen, until only the two humps added remain.\[P_\gamma = P_U + P_L + \gamma\,(\text{interference term})\]

γ = 1: indistinguishable records, full interference. γ = 0: distinguishable records, no interference. No one needs to read the record.

The record links the electron to the detector: "went through the upper slit" now goes together with "detector reads upper", and the same for the lower slit. The number γ measures how much the two detector readings overlap, that is, how hard they are to tell apart. If the readings can be told apart perfectly, γ = 0 and the bands disappear. If they cannot be told apart at all, γ = 1 and the bands are as strong as with no detector. In between, the bands fade. The readout gives the interference term as a percentage of its value with no detector. The app's which-slit detector is an ideal one: it always corresponds to γ = 0.

Predict first: What most directly removes the bands?

Details and math: the overlap γ

With a record, the state of electron and detector is ψU|DU⟩ + ψL|DL⟩. For records |DU⟩ and |DL⟩, the screen density is |ψU|² + |ψL|² + 2 Re[γ ψU*ψL] with γ = ⟨DU|DL⟩. Here γ is taken real and between 0 and 1. With γ < 1 the figure draws the crests of the stronger component at each point; the probabilities do not depend on this display choice.

5. Same pattern, three accounts ≈ 15 min

All three accounts start with the same prepared wavefunction and predict the same screen statistics. They disagree about what exists and what happens between the source and the screen. Choose an account above the demonstration; each starts a run.

What to notice: The predicted screen statistics are the same in all three; finite samples will fluctuate. In Orthodox, the packet ends in one hit. In Pilot Wave, a particle follows a definite path guided by the wave. In Many Worlds, watch the picture split into branches at each of the first three hits: each has its own hit, its own record and its own observer. Then one branch is followed, but the others are still there.

Orthodox

The wavefunction gives probabilities; no path is assigned. Measurement yields one randomly selected result, with collapse to the corresponding state.

Pilot Wave

A particle has a definite path through one slit, guided by a wave through both. Unknown initial positions account for the randomness.

Many Worlds

The wavefunction never collapses. Measurement correlates outcomes with different branches; an observer in each sees one result. Why branch weights give observed probabilities remains a central question.

These animations illustrate the accounts; the paths and branches are not directly observed here. Following one randomly sampled branch in Many Worlds is a display convention, not a physical collapse or a derivation of the Born rule.

In this symmetric Pilot Wave setup without a which-slit detector, trajectories do not cross the center line: an upper-half hit comes from the upper slit. With a detector, that rule need not hold.

Orthodox

The wavefunction is the complete description of the electron, and it gives probabilities for measurement results. No path is assigned between source and screen. At detection, one result occurs and the wavefunction is replaced by one that matches it. This step, often called collapse, is not described by the Schrödinger equation. A which-slit record counts as a measurement: the state is reduced to one slit's part, so nothing is left to interfere. Hard question: the theory does not say exactly when or why collapse happens, or what counts as a measurement. This is the measurement problem. In the app: no trajectories; the packet fades as hits are registered.

Pilot Wave

The electron is a particle with a definite position at all times. The wavefunction is also real, and it guides the particle. The particle goes through one slit; the wave goes through both, and the particle's motion depends on both parts. The randomness comes from not knowing the exact starting position. With a which-slit record, the detector's own parts end up in one of two clearly different states; the part of the wave tied to the other reading no longer affects the particle, so it is guided by one part alone. Hard question: the guidance is nonlocal: with several particles, the motion of one depends instantly on where the others are, however far away. In the app: each particle is drawn with its trajectory; only particles that get through a slit are shown.

Many Worlds

The wavefunction is everything, and it never collapses. When the electron reaches the screen, screen and electron become correlated: each possible hit position goes with a detector showing that hit. Each of these branches is real, with weight |ψ|². An observer in one branch sees one hit. A which-slit record splits the state into branches already at the wall; branches with different records cannot interfere. Hard question: if every outcome happens, why should the weights |ψ|² act as the probabilities we observe? This is the probability problem of Many Worlds. In the app: the split is shown in slow motion for the first three hits of a run; tick Slow motion to watch more.

In Pilot Wave the particles are coloured by the slit they passed. Without a detector, and with two equal slits as here, every cyan particle lands on the upper half of the screen and every orange one on the lower half: the trajectories never cross the center line. With the which-slit detector on, each particle is guided by one component alone and the rule breaks.

Pilot Wave, with no which-slit detector: an electron lands on the upper half of the screen in this symmetric setup. Which slit did it go through?

Details and math: guidance and branching

Pilot Wave. The particle moves forward at the packet speed; its sideways motion follows the phase of ψ:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\frac{\partial_y\psi}{\psi}\right),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.\]

This is the app's law, integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step of 2 nm. Starting positions are drawn from the source beam; at the wall a particle passes with probability T(y)², as in the app's default mode. The first published double-slit trajectories of this kind are in Philippidis, Dewdney and Hiley (1979). For photons, Kocsis and coworkers (2011) measured average paths with weak measurements. These are averages of the probability flow, which every account can compute; in Pilot Wave they are also the particle paths.

Many Worlds. Each of the 100 detector pixels defines a possible record, as in the app. After a run, the joint state of electron, screen and observer is a sum with one term for each record, weighted by pi = ∫pixel i|ψ|²:

\[\sum_i c_i\,|\text{hit at pixel } i\rangle\,|\text{observer sees } i\rangle,\qquad |c_i|^2=p_i .\]

To keep the picture readable, the split groups the 100 pixel records into 12 zones of 100 nm (6 zones of 200 nm on a phone); each miniature is one zone, drawn brighter when its weight is larger. The figure then follows one branch, chosen with probability equal to its weight, and adds its hit to that branch's record. With the which-slit detector on, the state already splits at the wall into a branch where the detector reads upper and one where it reads lower; the two branches cannot interfere.

6. The full equation: reflection and guided particles ≈ 15 min

Parts 1–5 used a simplified model: the wave only moves forward and the slits are soft openings that absorb the rest. N-Slits can also solve the Schrödinger equation itself on a grid, with a hard wall. Now part of the wave is reflected, and in Pilot Wave each particle is guided by the full computed wave.

What to notice: In front of the wall the incoming and reflected waves overlap. A guided particle never enters the wall; it bends where the phase of the wave bends. Particles started from |ψ|² stay distributed as |ψ|² as the wave evolves (equivariance).\[\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\frac{\mathbf{j}}{\rho}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi}\]

The wave passes through both slits, spreads and overlaps with itself; that much is standard quantum mechanics. Pilot-wave theory adds a particle with a definite position, moving with the velocity given by the wave: the probability current divided by the density, or equivalently the gradient of the phase. The particle goes through one slit, but the wave that guides it went through both, and interference distorts its phase fronts; that is how a single particle "senses" both slits.

One particle follows a single particle, packet after packet. Many particles sends hundreds at once, each guided by the same wave from a different starting point; the bands appear in the swarm. In these two set-ups only particles that get through the wall are shown (from the second packet on). Equivariance shows every particle, including those reflected or absorbed, coloured by where it started; compare the swarm with the brightness of the wave. Orthodox, reflecting wall shows the reflected wave without particles. The numerical engine needs WebGL2; on a slow computer it runs below the requested speed.

Predict first: particles are started from |ψ|² and each is guided by the wave. Later, how are they distributed?

Details and math: the guiding law and equivariance

The wave ψ(x, y, t) is the Schrödinger wave of standard quantum mechanics:

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(x,y) \right]\psi \]

The particle velocity is the probability current divided by the density ρ = |ψ|²:

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \frac{\mathbf{j}}{\rho}, \qquad \mathbf{j} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi\right) \]

Writing ψ = R eiS/ħ gives j = ρ∇S/m, so v = ∇S/m: the density says where particles are likely to be found, the phase S says which way they move.

Equivariance: ρ = |ψ|² and the particle density ρB obey the same continuity equation when the particles move with v = j/ρ,

\[ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}=0, \qquad \frac{\partial \rho_B}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho_B\mathbf{v})=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho_B(\mathbf{x},0)=|\psi(\mathbf{x},0)|^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \rho_B(\mathbf{x},t)=|\psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2 .\]

So if the starting positions are drawn from |ψ|², the positions found later are distributed as |ψ|², the same statistics the orthodox account gives for detections. The two accounts therefore agree on the statistics of positions; they differ in what they say exists between preparation and detection.

N-Slits' numerical engine uses a fourth-order finite-difference scheme on a grid with step λ/8; its Physics tab (Standard level) gives the details and the differences from the analytical model.

8. About the model, and going further

Parts 1–5 use N-Slits’ analytical engine: the beam moves forward at constant speed and spreads only sideways, and the slits are smooth openings that absorb what they do not let through. Part 6 uses its numerical engine, the Schrödinger equation on a grid with a hard wall.

The wavefunction is Ψ = F(x − vt) ei(kx − ωt) φ(y, x), with a packet envelope F that translates without spreading. The crests move at the phase velocity ω/k = v/2. The speed slider changes only how fast the display runs. Defaults: electron, λ = 50 nm, slit separation 300 nm, slit width σ = 30 nm, source width σ = 400 nm, packet length σ = 100 nm, source-to-wall 225 nm, wall-to-screen Z = 1000 nm. The brightness of the wave is enhanced so that weak parts stay visible. The full app's Physics tab (Standard level) gives the equations of both engines and their limits.
