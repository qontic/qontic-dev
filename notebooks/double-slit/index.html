Double Slit — Notebook

Electrons sent one at a time through two slits build up bands on a screen. Standard quantum mechanics predicts the pattern; Pilot Wave and Many Worlds reproduce the same predictions. They differ on what, if anything, can be said about what happens between the source and the screen.

About 40 minutes: Parts 1–4. Part 5 is optional. The demonstrations are the N-Slits app, embedded: each teal button sets it up and starts a run; you can also change the settings in its panel. Brief shows the demonstrations and questions; Full adds explanations and mathematics.

Reading the pictures

The experiment is seen from above: electrons come in from the left, the wall with the slits is in the middle, the screen is on the right. Each app keeps its own settings; Open in N-Slits ↗ opens the same set-up in the full app.

Red / blue: the wave ψ, coloured by its phase; brighter where it is strongerSky blue: slit 1 (upper)Orange: slit 2 (lower)Green curve: where hits are expected · bars: hits so far

ψ (psi): the wave. Phase: where in its cycle the wave is. Amplitude: how strong it is. Packet: one short burst of wave; each packet in the picture stands for a number of electrons sent one at a time. |ψ|²: the probability of a hit at a place (the Born rule). Defaults: wavelength λ = 50 nm, slit separation d = 300 nm, wall-to-screen distance Z = 1000 nm.

The brightness of the wave is enhanced so that weak parts stay visible; it is not an absolute probability scale. The stripes are the wave, not extra particles and not the bands counted on the screen.

1. Two slits are not one slit plus one slit ≈ 12 min

With one slit open, electrons spread into one broad hump. With both open the screen shows bands, including places that each slit alone reaches but that are now nearly empty. And each electron makes a single dot: the bands appear only after many electrons, each sent alone.

Predict first: you add the curve for slit 1 alone and the curve for slit 2 alone. What do you get?

What to notice: Compare Add the two humps with Both slits: opening the second slit makes some places less likely. Then press One electron at a time: each packet gives exactly one dot, yet after many dots the bands appear. The band spacing is close to\[\Delta y \approx \frac{\lambda Z}{d}\](λ the wavelength, Z the wall-to-screen distance, d the slit separation).

Add the two humps is what you get if the two single-slit probabilities simply add. N-Slits produces it by putting a which-slit detector on each slit; Part 3 explains why that gives exactly this sum. The spacing formula holds when the screen is far compared with the slit separation and the bands are close to the centre (small angles). Lowering λ, or moving the slits apart, brings the bands closer. Bands appear only where the two humps overlap; the panel below the controls shows the measured spacing.

Where a single dot lands cannot be predicted; only the frequencies can, through the Born rule P(y) ∝ |ψ(y)|². The electrons are sent one at a time, so the bands do not come from electrons interacting with each other. This was first done with electrons by Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi (1976) and, in a famous version, by Tonomura and coworkers (1989), both with an electron biprism in place of the two slits.

Details and math: where λ comes from

De Broglie's relation λ = h/p gives any particle with momentum p a wavelength. The default λ = 50 nm is a very slow electron. All lengths are in nanometres so that source, slits and screen fit in one picture; real electron experiments use other scales, with the same structure of bands. Bach and coworkers (2013) did the full experiment with electrons, opening and closing each slit with a movable mask.

2. Add, then square ≈ 8 min

At a point P on the screen, think of each slit's part of the wave as an arrow, like a clock hand: its length is the strength of that part, its direction the phase. Add the two arrows tip to tail; the probability is the length of the sum, squared.

What to notice: Drag the gold P along the screen, or use the two buttons. On the first dark band the arrows point nearly opposite ways and the sum is short. The panel under the picture gives the path difference r2 − r1 in wavelengths: about half a wavelength there.\[|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2=|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi_1^{*}\psi_2)\]

The dashed lines measure distances from the slits to P; they are not paths. The arrows are amplitudes, not directions of motion.

The arrows are the complex numbers ψ1(P) and ψ2(P). In the formula, the first two terms are the two humps of Part 1; the last, the interference term, can be positive or negative and makes the bands. Every extra wavelength of path turns an arrow once around, so bright bands sit where r2 − r1 is a whole number of wavelengths and dark bands halfway between.

The sum on the dark band is not zero here because the arrows have different lengths: at P = 83 nm, |ψ1|² ≈ 0.11 and |ψ2|² ≈ 0.40. Each slit's beam is narrow at the screen (about 130 nm) and lies mostly behind its own slit, so the two humps only partly overlap. Farther out one arrow is much shorter still and the bands fade; that is why only a few bands are bright.

Try it: on the first dark band, |ψ1 + ψ2|² is 0.10, not 0. Why?

Details and math: the arrows

The arrows are the two slit terms of the wave N-Slits computes (Gaussian slits, paraxial propagation), rotated together so that ψ1 points up; only their relative angle matters. Lengths are scaled so that the sum at the central peak, with all slits open, has length 1. The relative angle is close to 2π(r2 − r1)/λ, with small corrections from the curvature of the beams. With which-slit detectors on, the panel does not add the arrows (Part 3).

3. Which slit? A record at one slit ≈ 8 min

Put a detector at the upper slit that records whether the electron passed there. The record can be any physical change: a click, a flipped spin, a moved atom. Nobody needs to look at it.

Predict first: what most directly removes the bands?

What to notice: With the record, the bands disappear, behind the wall and on the screen, and the two humps added remain. The figure below shows a partial record: as the overlap γ of the two detector readings goes from 1 to 0, the bands fade.\[P_\gamma = P_1 + P_2 + \gamma\,(\text{interference term})\]

The record links electron and detector: "went through slit 1" goes with "detector reads 1", and the same for slit 2. γ measures how much the two detector readings overlap. If they can be told apart perfectly, γ = 0 and the bands disappear; if not at all, γ = 1 and the bands are as with no detector. For two equal humps, γ is the visibility of the bands relative to no record. The app's which-slit detector is ideal: γ = 0.

Record at the upper slit changes the colours. Behind the wall, the part of the wave that went through slit 1 (the detector fired) is drawn sky blue, the part through slit 2 (the detector stayed silent) orange; these two parts no longer interfere. The bars on the screen use the same colours: blue for electrons that fired the detector, orange for those that did not. Each colour alone is one hump; together they give the two humps added, with no bands.

A partial record (not in N-Slits), in a simple far-field model of the same two slits. overlap γ set by the slider; no record (γ = 1); reliable record (γ = 0).

Overlap of the records
Details and math: the overlap γ

With a record, the state of electron and detector is ψ1|D1⟩ + ψ2|D2⟩. The screen density is |ψ1|² + |ψ2|² + 2 Re[γ ψ1*ψ2] with γ = ⟨D1|D2⟩, here taken real between 0 and 1.

4. Same pattern, three views ≈ 15 min

All three start from the same wavefunction and predict the same screen statistics. Choose a view above the demonstration; each button starts a run. Switching view in a running app keeps the hits already recorded.

What to notice: In Orthodox, each hit is one detection; no path is drawn between source and screen. In Pilot Wave, each electron is a particle on a definite path, guided by the wave; look at the colour of each hit. In Many Worlds, one electron, the picture splits at each of the first three hits into one small copy for each place on the screen where the dot can land; one branch is then followed and the others continue. With 30 electrons per wave (Many Worlds) the split is not drawn: each hit multiplies the number of branches, shown as Branch records below the panel.

Orthodox

The standard formalism. The wavefunction gives the probabilities of measurement results. It assigns no path and does not say what happens between source and screen. At a measurement one result is obtained and the state is updated to match it (collapse). A which-slit record counts as a measurement. Open question: when a measurement happens, and what counts as one (the measurement problem).

Pilot Wave

The electron is a particle with a definite position at all times, guided by the wave. It goes through one slit; the wave goes through both. The randomness comes from not knowing the exact starting position. With a record, the part of the wave tied to the other reading no longer affects the particle. Open question: what the wave is; the theory gives particles a definite position but is explicitly nonlocal.

Many Worlds

The wavefunction is all there is, and it never collapses. At the screen each possible hit becomes correlated with a detector and an observer that see it; each branch has weight |ψ|², and an observer in each sees one result. A record splits the state into branches already at the wall; branches with different records cannot interfere. Open question: if every outcome happens, why the weights |ψ|² act as the probabilities we observe.

The animations illustrate each view; paths and branches are not observed. Following one branch in Many Worlds is a display choice, not a collapse.

Nonlocality is not special to Pilot Wave: Bell showed that any theory in which experiments have single definite outcomes and that reproduces the quantum predictions must be nonlocal in his sense; the Orthodox collapse acts on the whole wave at once. Many Worlds avoids Bell's conclusion because measurements do not have single outcomes. Spontaneous-collapse theories (GRW, CSL) are different again: they change the Schrödinger equation, predict small deviations, and are being tested; they are not covered here.

All three views: which statement do they all accept?

Pilot Wave, no detector: an electron lands on the upper half of the screen. Which slit did it go through?

Details and math: guidance and branching

Pilot Wave. The particle moves forward at the packet speed; its sideways motion follows the phase of ψ:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\frac{\partial_y\psi}{\psi}\right),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.\]

Starting positions are drawn from the source beam. The first published double-slit trajectories of this kind are in Philippidis, Dewdney and Hiley (1979). For photons, Kocsis and coworkers (2011) measured average paths with weak measurements: averages of the probability flow, which every view can compute; in Pilot Wave they are also the particle paths.

Many Worlds. Each detector pixel defines a possible record. After a run the state of electron, screen and observer is a sum with one term per record, weighted by pi = ∫pixel i|ψ|²:

\[\sum_i c_i\,|\text{hit at pixel } i\rangle\,|\text{observer sees } i\rangle,\qquad |c_i|^2=p_i .\]

With one electron per wave the split shows one small copy per pixel, brighter when its weight is larger, and then follows one, chosen with probability equal to its weight; the wave of the followed branch ends at its hit. With the which-slit detector on, the state already splits at the wall into a branch where the detector reads 1 and one where it reads 2.

5. Optional: the full equation ≈ 10 min

Parts 1–4 used a simplified model: the wave only moves forward and the slits are soft openings. N-Slits can also solve the Schrödinger equation on a grid, with a hard wall. Part of the wave is then reflected, and in Pilot Wave each particle is guided by the full computed wave.

What to notice: In front of the wall the incoming and reflected waves overlap. Many particles sends hundreds of particles, each guided by the same wave from a different start; the bands form in the swarm, and paths do not cross the centre line. Equivariance shows every particle, coloured by where it started: particles started from |ψ|² stay distributed as |ψ|² as the wave moves. Orthodox, reflecting wall shows the reflected wave without particles.

The numerical engine needs WebGL2 and is slow on weak computers: a packet can take a minute to reach the screen. A few particles are counted as absorbed at the wall; that is an effect of the finite time step, not of the guidance law.

Predict first: particles are started from |ψ|² and each is guided by the wave. Later, how are they distributed?

Details and math: the guiding law and equivariance

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(x,y) \right]\psi, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \frac{\mathbf{j}}{\rho}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi} \]

Writing ψ = R eiS/ħ gives v = ∇S/m. ρ = |ψ|² and the particle density ρB obey the same continuity equation when the particles move with v = j/ρ, so ρB = |ψ|² at the start implies ρB = |ψ|² at all later times. The engine uses a fourth-order finite-difference scheme on a grid of step λ/8; the full app's Physics tab (Standard level) gives the details.

7. The model and references

Parts 1–4 use N-Slits' analytical engine: the beam moves forward at constant speed and spreads only sideways, and the slits are smooth openings that absorb what they do not let through. Part 5 uses its numerical engine.

The wavefunction is Ψ = F(x − vt) ei(kx − ωt) φ(y, x), with a packet envelope F that moves without spreading; the crests move at the phase velocity ω/k = v/2. Defaults: electron, λ = 50 nm, slit separation 300 nm, slit width σ = 30 nm, source width σ = 400 nm, packet length σ = 100 nm, source-to-wall 225 nm, Z = 1000 nm. The full app's Physics tab gives the equations of both engines and their limits. Going further: N-Slits (up to 8 slits, both engines, three levels); the Delayed choice notebook.
