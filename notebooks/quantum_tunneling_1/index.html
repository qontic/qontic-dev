1. A classical particle with too little energy reflects

Classically, a particle with total energy below the barrier height cannot cross the barrier. It reaches the first edge and reflects: T=0.

What to notice: A finite quantum barrier can transmit part of the wave even when the classical prediction is zero. Wider and higher barriers usually transmit less.\[T=\text{transmitted fraction}\]

Throughout this notebook, ℏ = m = 1. The central momentum is k0, so the incident central energy is E0 = k02/2. The rendered state is a normalizable Gaussian packet, not a literal infinite plane wave.

Define transmission before comparing models.\[T=\frac{N_{\mathrm{right}}}{N_{\mathrm{incident}}}\]For a classical particle with E0<V0, the prediction is Tclassical=0. For a finite quantum barrier, the calculated transmission can be nonzero.

2. The Schrödinger equation evolves a complex packet

The initial state is a Gaussian packet moving to the right. The visualization solves the one-dimensional time-dependent Schrödinger equation numerically with the same square barrier shown above.

What to notice: The packet spreads as it moves. At the barrier, it separates into reflected and transmitted parts. The gold curve shows where detection is more likely.

Re ψ, Im ψ, and ρ(x,t)=|ψ|² are overlaid in one shared spatial plot. Their vertical display scales are fixed for the entire run, so spreading and attenuation remain visible. The cyan/blue wavefunction curves and the gold density curve use different fixed display normalizations, so compare their shapes and evolution rather than their plotted heights. Re ψ and Im ψ are useful components of a complex state, but neither is separately observable: a global phase rotation changes them while leaving ρ, j, and all detector probabilities unchanged.

The full equation comes first.\[i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi\]The first term describes how the state changes and spreads when it is free. The second term inserts the orange barrier. The simulation then calculates the gold probability density from ρ=|ψ|².

3. Below the barrier, the local wave is evanescent

For a stationary component with E<V₀, the wave number inside the barrier becomes imaginary: k→iκ. The resulting spatial dependence is exponential rather than oscillatory.

What to notice: The wave becomes smaller inside the barrier, but it does not immediately become zero. If some reaches the far edge, a transmitted wave continues beyond it.

This does not mean the packet vanishes at the first edge. Because the barrier is finite, the wavefunction can remain nonzero at the far boundary. Matching the solution across both boundaries produces a transmitted component beyond the barrier.

Follow the below-barrier chain.\[E<V_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=i\kappa\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{exponential behavior inside the barrier}\]“Evanescent” describes the form of the wavefunction inside the barrier. It does not mean that the particle stops at a classical turning point or that energy is borrowed.
κ positive decay constantk wave numberA, B coefficients fixed by both barrier edges

For each stationary plane-wave component, a finite square barrier has an exact transmission coefficient. In the below-barrier regime emphasized here, transmission becomes exponentially small for a thick or high barrier, but is not generically zero.

5. One detection at a time; a frequency after many runs

Do not read the final split packet as many particles in one experiment. First prepare one identical packet, then record one localized detector position. Repeating that preparation builds a frequency record.

How to read the results: Each dot is one detector event from one run. After many runs, the fraction detected on each side approaches the probability predicted by the wavefunction.

Reading the canvas from top to bottom: (1) the gold late-time density specifies the probability of a detector position; (2) every red or green dot is one independent experimental run sampled from that density; (3) the lower bars compare the observed fractions with the quantum prediction. A dot is not a particle trajectory and the collection of dots is not one simultaneous multi-particle snapshot.

The notebook represents an ideal position measurement by sampling from the late-time probability distribution. It does not simulate the detector’s microscopic dynamics.

6. Shared predictions; different accounts of the unmeasured system

Both choices use the same Schrödinger evolution, barrier potential, probability density, current, and detector statistics. They differ in what they say, if anything, about a particle configuration between preparation and detection.

The main difference: Both accounts predict the same results here. The pilot-wave account adds a definite particle position and guided path; the standard measurement account (orthodox) does not assign that path.

Interpretation note

Standard measurement account (orthodox)

ψ is a probability amplitude. It evolves unitarily before measurement; a detector event is localized and has probabilities given by the Born rule. This presentation does not add a definite, measurement-independent particle path to the state description. Re ψ and Im ψ are display components whose split depends on a global phase convention.

Pilot-wave / Bohmian mechanics

The pilot-wave account includes both ψ and one actual particle configuration X(t) in each run. For a spinless one-dimensional particle, the guiding law is dX/dt = j/ρ, where j=Im(ψ*∂xψ) in ℏ=m=1 units. The gold curves show a sampled equilibrium ensemble of configurations, obtained by the equivalent 1D cumulative-probability construction; they are not multiple particles in one run and not classical guesses.

Questions to carry forward

The goal is a workable understanding of what the model predicts before taking a position on the interpretation.

Does tunneling mean energy is not conserved?

No. The time-independent barrier has a conserved energy. The finite barrier changes the spatial form of the state and allows a nonzero transmission amplitude. “Borrowing energy” is not the explanation used in this notebook.

Why can one detector click even though the wavefunction extends across space?

The wavefunction supplies probability amplitudes. A position-sensitive detector produces one localized event in a single run. Repeating the same preparation reveals the probability pattern predicted by |ψ|².

Is the quantum result a small correction to the classical result?

No. Classical mechanics gives a single route from the initial conditions. Quantum mechanics first evolves a state and then gives probabilities for possible outcomes. The classical limit is recovered only under special conditions.

What does the trajectory picture resolve?

It offers one way to speak about a definite configuration during the experiment while preserving the same detector statistics. It does not remove the need for the wavefunction; in the pilot-wave account the wave guides the configuration.

Questions to carry forward

  • Tunneling does not require the particle to borrow energy.
  • One run produces one localized detector event.
  • The two interpretations shown here predict the same detector statistics.
