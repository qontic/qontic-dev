1. Begin with the classical oscillator

A mass on a spring is the classical starting point. The object has a definite position, moves through equilibrium, and turns around where all of its energy is stored as potential energy.

This section sets the reference picture for the rest of the notebook. The quantum oscillator keeps the same parabolic potential, but replaces one definite classical trajectory with a wavefunction and measurement probabilities.

2. The quantum oscillator is described by a wavefunction

The quantum oscillator is not described by one definite classical position as it evolves. It is described by a wavefunction Ψ(x,t), and the gold curve |Ψ|² predicts where position measurements are likely to occur.

This notebook uses dimensionless classroom units with ℏ=1 in the simulation. The displayed equation keeps the standard symbols so the model remains connected to the notation used in a modern physics course.

Solution for one energy state

For the harmonic oscillator, the separated solutions have this form:

Ψn(x,t)=NnHn(ξ)e−ξ2/2e−iEnt/ℏ
Here ξ=x/ℓ, ℓ=√[ℏ/(mω)], Hn is a Hermite polynomial, and Nn normalizes the state.

The polynomial Hn creates the nodes of excited states. The Gaussian factor makes the wavefunction fade at large |x|. The final phase factor rotates Re Ψ and Im Ψ in time, but for one energy state it does not change |Ψ|².

3. The allowed energies form an equally spaced ladder

The quantum harmonic oscillator cannot have just any energy. Its energy levels are evenly spaced, and the lowest allowed energy is above the bottom of the potential.

The bottom of the orange parabola is set to V=0. The first horizontal line is not at zero because the oscillator still has zero-point energy in its lowest quantum state.

4. Zero-point energy is the cost of being confined

The lowest quantum oscillator state is not a motionless point at the bottom of the parabola. The ground state has a finite spread in position, and that spread is tied to momentum uncertainty.

The educational goal of this section is to connect the gold ground-state width to the uncertainty principle. The oscillator cannot make both its position spread and momentum spread vanish at the same time.

Use the spread to understand the nonzero ground-state energy.ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2,   E0=ℏω/2If the position distribution is squeezed, the momentum uncertainty increases. The ground state balances kinetic-energy cost and potential-energy cost.

Connect uncertainty to zero-point energy

A rough energy estimate is

E ≈ (Δp)²/(2m) + ½mω²(Δx)²

If Δx is made too small, the kinetic-energy term grows. If Δx is too large, the potential-energy term grows. The lowest balance gives E₀=ℏω/2.

5. Superpositions can move probability density

A single energy state has a stationary density. A superposition of two energy states has a changing relative phase, so the probability density can move, breathe, or shift in time.

A coherent-state-like packet is included because it is the quantum state in this notebook that most closely resembles classical simple harmonic motion: the center oscillates while the packet remains spread out.

6. One measurement gives one position; many runs reveal |Ψ|²

The wavefunction gives probabilities, not a list of guaranteed future positions. A single preparation followed by a position measurement gives one localized result.

The point of this section is to separate one event from the distribution. The top curve is the predicted density; the lower detector record is built from many repeated, identically prepared trials.

7. Orthodox and pilot-wave accounts use the same wavefunction

The two accounts shown here use the same Schrödinger evolution and the same |Ψ|² measurement predictions. They differ in what they say about the system between preparation and measurement.

The harmonic oscillator is especially useful for this comparison because a real stationary energy state has zero probability current. In a pilot-wave account, stationary current means stationary configurations, even though the wavefunction still has a time-dependent complex phase.

Keep the prediction and the interpretation separate.|Ψ|² predicts position statistics   |   v=j/|Ψ|² is the pilot-wave guidance ruleThe standard account stops at the measurement probabilities. The pilot-wave account adds a definite configuration guided by the same wavefunction.

Interpretation note

Standard account

Ψ is a probability amplitude. The Born rule says |Ψ|² gives the density of possible position outcomes. This account does not assign a definite unmeasured path between measurements.

Pilot-wave / Bohmian account

Ψ guides a definite configuration through v=j/|Ψ|². For a real stationary oscillator state, j=0, so configurations remain still. Superpositions and coherent packets can have nonzero guidance motion.
