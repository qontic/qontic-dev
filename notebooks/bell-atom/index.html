Bell's atom: why moving things contract

In 1976 John Bell asked how special relativity should be taught so that students could answer his spaceship question. His suggestion was the older road of FitzGerald, Larmor, Lorentz and Poincaré: stay in one frame, and ask what happens to a piece of matter when it is set moving. This notebook walks that road with a simulation of the atom Bell used.

1. The puzzle

Two ships, one thread, identical engines.

Ships B and C float at rest a distance L apart, joined by a fragile thread that is just long enough. A light flash from A, halfway between them, reaches both at the same moment, and both fire identical engines running the same program. Seen from A, the ships always have the same speed, so the gap between them stays L. Does the thread break?

Bell put this to colleagues at CERN; by his account, an informal canvass of the Theory Division gave a clear consensus that it would not. It does, as Dewan and Beran had already shown.6 Try it in the Bell's Spaceships app, or answer here first.

In A's frame the gap stays L. What happens to the thread?

2. The field of a moving charge

Matter is held together by electric forces. Start with a single charge.

At rest, the field of a charge is the same in every direction, and its surfaces of equal potential are spheres. Set the charge moving at speed v and Maxwell's equations give a different field: the surfaces of equal potential become ellipsoids shorter by the factor 1/γ along the motion, where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). The field gets weaker ahead and behind and stronger at the sides. Heaviside worked this out in 1889.2 In the same year FitzGerald proposed that bodies moving through the ether are shortened along their motion, to explain the null result of Michelson and Morley.3

What to notice: raise the speed. The equal-potential curves (rings) flatten by exactly 1/γ. The arrows still point straight away from the charge, but they crowd toward the sides.

Details and math

For a charge q moving with constant speed v along z, at the point (x, y, z) measured from its present position (Gaussian units),

E = q γ r / (x² + y² + γ² z²)3/2,   B = (v/c) × E,   φ = q γ / (x² + y² + γ² z²)1/2.

Surfaces of constant φ satisfy x² + y² + γ²z² = constant: ellipsoids whose axis along the motion is 1/γ of the others. This is Bell's equation (1).1

3. Bell's atom

An electron orbiting a nucleus that is pushed gently to high speed.

Bell's simplest piece of matter is a classical atom: one electron going round a much heavier nucleus. Start it at rest with a circular orbit, then push the nucleus gently until it moves at speed v. Follow the electron with the field of section 2, the Lorentz force, and Lorentz's relation between momentum and velocity, p = mv/√(1 − v²/c²). Bell's result: the orbit becomes an ellipse shorter by 1/γ along the motion, and it goes round more slowly, by the factor γ.1

Nothing in this calculation refers to a moving observer, to clocks being synchronized, or to the relativity of simultaneity. It is one frame, Maxwell's equations and relativistic mechanics.

What to notice: the orbit, drawn around the nucleus, flattens as the speed rises. Compare the measured ratio with 1/γ, and the measured period with γ. Both are read from the last complete revolution.

How the push is applied, and why it matters

Bell applied the force to the nucleus only and let the electron follow. In this purely classical model that choice causes a problem: any steady force on a Kepler orbit slowly changes its eccentricity, by an amount that does not shrink when the push is made slower. The orbit would end up eccentric for reasons that have nothing to do with relativity. Untick the box to see it.

The notebook therefore pushes both particles in proportion to their mass, as a uniform gravitational field would. Then, at low speeds, the atom is simply carried along, and whatever happens at high speed comes from the electromagnetic and relativistic dynamics alone. Bell himself stressed that the contraction is expected only for gentle acceleration of a system held together by its internal forces.1

The equations solved, with the nucleus at zN(t) moving at V(t): dp/dt = −e(E + v × B/c) + m d(γVV)/dt ẑ, v = p/√(m² + p²/c²), with E and B the uniform-motion field of section 2 at the current speed V(t). Fourth-order Runge–Kutta, 300 steps per orbit.

4. From atoms to a thread

If each atom is shorter, so is anything made of them.

A thread is a chain of atoms held in place by the same kind of forces. Bell argues that any group of charges confined to a region of length L at rest is, once set gently moving, confined to a region shorter by 1/γ.1 So the thread's natural length at speed v is L/γ. In the spaceship problem the engines hold its ends L apart: it is stretched by γ − 1, and it snaps if that is more than it can take.

Bell adds a proviso. If the thread were strong and the ships light, the thread would pull the ships together instead, and the whole system would contract without breaking. The spaceship question assumes the opposite: ships far too massive for the thread to move.

What to notice: the atoms' orbits (ellipses) flatten with speed and want to sit closer together; the bonds between them (springs) are pulled long. Switch to a strong thread and the ships come together instead.

5. The moving observer

Her rulers and clocks are made of atoms too.

Now imagine an observer travelling with the atom. Her rulers are made of atoms, so they are shorter by 1/γ. Her clocks are atoms too, so they run slow by γ. She sets her clocks in agreement by light signals, assuming light takes as long to go one way as the other, as it would if she were at rest. Bell shows that the coordinates she then uses are exactly

t′ = γ(t − vz/c²),   z′ = γ(z − vt),   x′ = x,

the Lorentz transformation. In her coordinates the moving atom is a circle that goes round in the rest period, exactly like an atom at rest. Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force keep the same form in these variables, so nothing she can measure tells her she is moving.1

What to notice: the same electron, after the push, in two sets of variables. Left, A's: a flattened orbit that goes round slowly; her ruler (white bar) is shortened with it. Right, hers: a circle with the rest period, and a ruler of full length.

Both pictures use the same simulated electron (section 3's model after the push). The right one is the left one transformed, event by event.

Details and math

During coasting the nucleus moves as z = c₀ + vt. Its primed position, z′ = γ(c₀ + vt − vt) = γc₀, is constant: in her variables the nucleus is at rest. Each electron event (t, x, z) is transformed with the formulas above; the orbit is drawn about the nucleus. The small departure from a perfect circle and from the rest period (about 1–2%) comes from the finite length of the push in the simulation.

The inverse transformation has the same form with v replaced by −v. So A, seen from her coordinates, has short rulers and slow clocks too. Each can account for the other's measurements, and neither can show the other to be the one really moving.1

6. Lorentz or Einstein?

Two roads to the same predictions.

The longer road (Lorentz, Larmor, Poincaré)

Starts from: the laws of physics in one frame, such as Maxwell's equations and the forces holding matter together.

Gets: contraction and slow clocks as effects of those forces on moving matter, case by case. Moving observers are then shown to be unable to detect their motion.

Easy questions: the thread. It is asked in one frame about one object, and that frame answers it directly.

The shorter road (Einstein, 1905)

Starts from: the principle that the laws look the same to every uniformly moving observer, and the constancy of the speed of light.

Gets: the Lorentz transformation at once, for every kind of matter and force, without a model of either.

Easy questions: anything symmetric between frames. The thread needs care, because the ships do not stay at rest in one inertial frame.

Bell judged that the facts of physics do not force either philosophy. He valued Einstein's approach for its power and elegance, and argued that students should also be taken along the longer road, because it shows that the laws in one frame account for everything, including what moving observers see, and builds the instincts that the spaceship question tests.1

The dynamical account has limits that Bell himself noted. Classical electromagnetism cannot explain why atoms are stable, and real matter is held together by forces it does not include; the argument has to assume that all of them share the same symmetry. Brown and Pooley put the point sharply: the argument shows that contraction follows if the laws of matter have Lorentz symmetry, but not why they have it.4,5 Which road gives the explanation remains a live question among philosophers of physics.

For Q-Ontic, this is a case where two accounts agree on every prediction and tell different stories about what happens. Quantum mechanics offers a similar situation, with one difference: there, some proposals, such as spontaneous-collapse models, do predict different results.
