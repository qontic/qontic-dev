Before you begin: compare the picture, prediction, and result

This notebook asks a focused experimental question: if Earth moved through a stationary light-carrying ether, should an interferometer rotate into and out of a measurable fringe shift?

1. The apparatus

two light paths

A beam splitter sends light along two perpendicular arms. The returning beams recombine at the detector and form an interference signal.

2. The classical prediction

Δt, ΔN

The ether-wind model predicts a small orientation-dependent time difference and therefore a rotation-dependent fringe shift.

3. The experimental lesson

null result

The key result is not an empty screen. It is the absence of the specific rotation-dependent shift predicted by the ether model.

1. Start with the interferometer

A Michelson interferometer splits one light beam into two perpendicular arms, reflects both beams from mirrors, and recombines them at a detector. The detector does not compare the arms separately; it records the interference made by the two returning waves.

First goal: identify what is being compared. The experiment compares the round-trip light travel along two perpendicular arms, then asks whether rotating the apparatus changes the interference signal.

2. What would an ether wind predict?

Before special relativity, many physicists imagined light as a wave moving through a medium called the ether. If Earth moved through that medium, then the arm parallel to the ether wind and the arm perpendicular to it should have slightly different round-trip travel times.

Prediction to test: the classical ether model predicts a small but measurable time difference. The modern lesson is that this predicted effect was not observed.

Use now: the size of the ether prediction.Δt ≈ Lv²/c³A longer arm or a faster hypothetical ether speed makes the predicted time difference larger. The dependence on v² means the sign of the ether wind speed does not matter here.

Show the round-trip time formulas

In the classical ether model, the parallel and perpendicular round-trip times are:

tparallel = L/(c−v) + L/(c+v)
tperpendicular = 2L/√(c²−v²)

For v much smaller than c, their difference is approximately Δt ≈ Lv²/c³.

3. A path difference becomes a phase difference

The detector responds to phase, not to travel time directly. If one returning wave arrives a little ahead of the other, the bright and dark fringes shift.

Beginner bridge: read the chain as path difference → phase difference → fringe shift. This is the same wave logic used in interference experiments, now applied to the two arms of the interferometer.

4. Rotate the apparatus and look for a changing fringe shift

If the ether-wind picture were correct, rotating the interferometer should swap which arm is more parallel to the wind. That should change the predicted time difference and move the fringe pattern.

Important distinction: the test is not whether fringes exist. Fringes can exist in any interferometer. The ether prediction is a change in fringe position as the apparatus rotates.

5. The null result: what was not seen?

The experiment did not find the rotation-dependent fringe shift expected from motion through a stationary ether. This challenged the idea that light needed a mechanical medium with a preferred rest frame.

Define “null” carefully: it does not mean there was no light or no interference. It means the specific predicted change—the orientation-dependent ether-wind fringe shift—was absent within experimental sensitivity.

6. The modern relativity reading

Special relativity replaces the ether-wind expectation with a new principle: the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers. The Michelson-Morley result is one important clue that the older ether-wind picture needed revision.

Careful reading: the experiment alone is not the full derivation of special relativity. It is a powerful test because it removes a predicted effect from the classical ether model.

Keep the two accounts separate.ether model: light speed changes relative to ether   |   relativity: vacuum light speed is cThe modern account does not need a preferred ether rest frame. The null result is expected because the predicted ether-wind speed difference is not part of the theory.

Careful historical note

The experiment alone is not the entire derivation of special relativity. It is one important result, together with Maxwell’s equations and other evidence, that made the old ether-wind picture difficult to keep.

The Lorentz-contraction idea was historically introduced before Einstein’s 1905 interpretation. Special relativity later made length contraction and time dilation consequences of spacetime structure rather than mechanical effects of motion through an ether.

7. What conceptual difficulty did the experiment address?

The Michelson-Morley experiment turned a philosophical-sounding question—“What is light moving relative to?”—into a measurable prediction. The missing rotation shift helped move physics away from a preferred ether frame.

Use the selector to compare three accounts.

Classical ether account

Light is treated as a wave in a mechanical medium. Motion through that medium should create an orientation-dependent travel-time difference, so rotating the apparatus should move the fringes.

Experimental null result

The predicted orientation-dependent fringe shift was not found. The lesson is specific: the ether-wind signal was absent, not that interference itself disappeared.

Modern relativity account

The laws of physics do not require a preferred rest frame for light. In inertial frames, the vacuum speed of light is c, so the classical ether-wind shift is not expected.
