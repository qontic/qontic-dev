In 1922 Stern and Gerlach sent silver atoms through a magnet whose field is stronger on one side. Each atom is a tiny magnet (its spin), and instead of a smear the atoms landed in two separate spots. Here you set the spin direction and the magnet's direction, and the sphere predicts how many land in each spot. Pilot Wave shows each atom as a dot guided by the wave; Orthodox shows only the wave and, at the screen, where atoms are detected. XZ looks at the screen face-on.

Point the spin, turn the magnet, and compare the dots at the screen with the expected split.

Physics and model limits

What you see

The axes match the textbook field of Griffiths and Schroeter (below), which depends on x and z only: the beam travels along y, z is up, and x points across the beam (x, y, z right-handed). The app opens in a 3D view; the YZ button gives the side view, with the beam moving to the right along y and z up, and XZ looks along the beam. The cloud is the total density of the two-component spinor:

ρ(x,t) = Ψ†Ψ

In the pilot-wave reading the dots are possible positions of the particle, one per run. They start at random positions drawn from |Ψ|² and then follow the guidance law below. The dots and the cloud are coloured by the spin of the wave along the axis of the magnet the packet has reached, (Ψ†σΨ)·n/Ψ†Ψ, as in the comparative Stern–Gerlach app: pale blue before the first magnet and wherever that spin has no definite sign along n (the local spin points across the axis), green where it points along +n and red along −n. For the default +x spin the whole packet stays pale blue in the magnet (its spin turns about z, at right angles to n) until the two parts separate and turn green and red. A +z packet turns green as soon as it enters a z magnet. Display: Outcome colours switches to blue for + and orange for − (pale grey before the field, grey field lines), which stay distinct for red–green colour blindness; the counts in the corner also mark + with ▲ and − with ▼. A dot stops when it reaches the screen, and the counts in the corner are made from those dots. The wave itself passes through the screen and keeps evolving. In the orthodox reading there are no dots; when the packet reaches the screen the app draws one detection per run, at a position chosen at random from |Ψ|² there (see Two readings).

The spin can point in any direction, set by two angles: θ from +z, and φ about z from +x toward +y (the beam). The spinor is (cos θ/2, eiφ sin θ/2) in the ±z basis. The default, θ = 90° and φ = 0, points along +x, across the beam: an equal, in-phase superposition of +z and −z. In a z magnet the two spin components separate vertically. In the pilot-wave reading each particle ends up in one branch, and which one is fixed by where it started: dots from the upper half of the packet go up, dots from the lower half go down; only dots that start very near the middle can end in the other branch. In the orthodox reading there is no particle in a branch before the detection; the detection finds it in one region, with the probabilities given by |Ψ|².

Display: Show phase (Expert level) colours the cloud by the phase of the wave. The fast oscillation along the beam, ei(ky − ωt), is left out, because it is shorter than the spacing of the drawn points; what is shown is the phase relative to the beam's plane wave.

Magnets

The packet starts in a field-free region, 2.1 cm before the magnet, passes the 3.5 cm magnet, and drifts to the screen. Screen distance sets how far the screen stands after the end of the last magnet (0.7 to 4.2 cm); after the magnet the two parts keep the sideways speed they gained, so a farther screen shows a larger splitting. The Magnets control chooses the field:

  • One magnet: a single magnet, 3.5 cm long.
  • Two magnets: a short magnet, a field-free gap, then a second magnet at its own angle from the first (Second magnet, from the first: 90° by default; at Magnet angle 0, a z magnet then an x magnet). A +z packet first moves up as a whole, then splits into +x and −x; the equal superposition ends in four spots. Off the axis the second magnet's field is slightly tilted, so its split is not exactly as predicted. Block after magnet 1 stops the + or the − part in the gap: when the packet's centre passes the middle of the gap, the wave on that side of the axis is set to zero (with an edge three cells wide) and the dots there are removed and counted as stopped; in the orthodox reading each run passes the block with the probability of the unblocked part. This acts on the whole packet at once, as the fast engine treats the packet's motion along the beam. The first magnet is short, so the two parts are only partly apart at the block: its edge cuts through the overlap, and the passing part spreads a little more (diffraction at the edge) than without the block. With a prepared +z spin it reproduces the classic sequence z, x, z: set Magnet angle 90° (first magnet along x), Second magnet 270° from the first (second along z), block the − part, and half of the passing atoms end at −z.
  • Weak field: one magnet whose uniform part is small, so the field nearly vanishes on one side of the box. There the spin cannot follow the field and flips; one spot is distorted and the counts are no longer 50/50.

Edit geometry (the pencil in the toolbar, or right-click the canvas) shows handles on the canvas: drag them to move the magnet's start and end and the screen along the beam, or turn the magnet about the beam. You can also drag on the small sphere next to the spin sliders to point the spin. Each change restarts the packet; Reset geometry returns to the 1922-like layout.

The gradient slider stops where the parts would reach the walls of the box, out to 2.5 standard deviations of |Ψ|². The limit depends on the magnets and the screen distance: the wave spreads as it travels, so a farther screen allows a smaller gradient. With two magnets the limit uses the farthest expected spot. The dots, the detections and the weights of |Ψ|² are counted at the nearest expected spot (from the classical estimate below). Choosing a magnet setting sets the gradient to 90% of the limit; moving the screen lowers the gradient only if it is above the new limit. When the expected splitting is less than four standard deviations, the line under the controls says that the spots overlap.

The Magnet angle turns every magnet about the beam axis, from +z toward +x: 90° measures spin along +x, 180° along −z. The magnet can only turn about the beam: the beam has to pass between the poles, and a gradient along the beam would speed the atoms up or slow them down without splitting them. A magnet whose axis makes an angle γ with the spin sends the fraction cos²(γ/2) to its + side; the sphere next to the controls shows γ and this prediction. For example, a +z spin in a magnet turned 45° gives cos² 22.5° ≈ 85% and 15%.

Spin arrows

Display: Spin arrow draws the spin ⟨σ⟩ of the whole state at the packet (described below), in both readings; Spin on dots adds, in the pilot-wave reading, a small arrow on each dot. Each small arrow sits on a dot and points along the spin direction of the wave at the dot's position, Ψ†σΨ/Ψ†Ψ. For a single particle this is a unit vector at every point, so the arrows always have full length. Before the magnet all arrows point along the prepared spin; once the parts separate, the arrows in each part point along + or − the magnet axis, and dots in the region where the parts still overlap show intermediate directions. In the minimal version of pilot-wave theory, presented for example by Norsen, only the position is a property of the particle, so the arrow is a property of the wave at the particle; Dewdney, Holland and Kyprianidis instead treat it as the particle's spin, carried along its trajectory.

T. Norsen, “The pilot-wave perspective on spin,” Am. J. Phys. 82, 337–348 (2014), arXiv:1305.1280; C. Dewdney, P. R. Holland and A. Kyprianidis, “What happens in a spin measurement?,” Phys. Lett. A 119, 259–267 (1986).

In the orthodox reading there is one arrow, at the centre of |Ψ|²: the expectation value ⟨σ⟩ of the whole state. Its length is 1 while the spin has a definite direction, that is, while the state is an eigenstate of some spin component. As the two parts separate, spin and position become entangled and the arrow shrinks; for an equal split it goes to zero: the spin alone then has no definite direction, only the probabilities of the two outcomes. Each part on its own has a definite spin along the magnet axis.

Inside the magnet the arrow turns about the field direction: the part of the spin across B precesses at the Larmor frequency, while the part along B stays fixed. For the default +x spin in a z magnet the arrow therefore spins around the z axis in the x–y plane, and shrinks as the parts separate. In the model one turn takes about 3 µs on the clock (much faster for a real silver atom: about 0.4 ns in a 0.1 T field). The arrow is redrawn a few times per second, so its direction appears to jump. A spin along the magnet axis does not precess.

Two readings

The View switch in the controls (Pilot Wave, Orthodox) changes between two readings of the same wave. The wave, the probabilities and the statistics at the screen are the same in both; they differ in what they say happens in a single run. A third reading, Many Worlds, is in the three-view Stern–Gerlach app.

Where the measurement happens. Neither the magnet alone nor the screen alone measures the spin. The magnet entangles the spin with the position: the +n part of the wave goes one way and the −n part the other. The screen then records a position, and the side of the screen tells which part it came from. Magnet and screen together make the spin measurement.

Orthodox reading. Only the wave is described: the theory gives the particle no position before it is detected, and |Ψ|² gives the probability of a detection at each place on the screen (the Born rule). In the standard account (Dirac, von Neumann) a measurement also collapses the wave onto the outcome found: after a detection in the upper half the state is the upper part alone. The theory does not say which physical interactions count as a measurement, or when the collapse happens; this is the measurement problem (Bell, "Against 'measurement'"). The app draws the wave without collapse so that many runs can be shown on one screen: when the packet reaches the screen it draws one detection per run, at a position chosen at random from |Ψ|² there, and the box in the corner counts them. While the packet travels, the box shows the weight of |Ψ|² in each region of the screen (two halves along the magnet's axis, or four quadrants after two magnets); at the screen these are the chances of a detection there. When the arrow ⟨σ⟩ has shrunk to zero, spin and position are entangled; the spin alone is then in an "improper" mixture, which is not the same as each atom having a definite but unknown spin.

Pilot-wave reading. The particle has a position at all times and is guided by the wave; the dots are possible positions, one per run, started at random from |Ψ|² (de Broglie, Bohm). No collapse is needed: once the two parts of the wave are apart, the part without the particle no longer affects its motion. Each particle ends up in one branch, fixed by where it started. The screen is idealised: it stops and records the dot and leaves the wave unchanged; a real detector is itself a quantum system.

Contextuality. In the pilot-wave reading the result of a "spin measurement" is not a value the particle carries in advance; it depends on the arrangement. For the default +x spin, dots that start above the middle (apart from a few very near it) end in the upper spot whatever the magnet's polarity. At Magnet angle 0° the upper spot is the +z result; at 180° the magnet's axis points down, the field is stronger toward −z, and the same upper spot is the −n result. Set Display: Dot colour to Starting half (Standard level) and compare 0° and 180°: the yellow dots land in the upper spot both times, recorded once as + and once as −.

Geometry

The canvas shows one tunnel from just behind the starting packet (y = 0) to the screen. The wave is computed in two parts. Along the beam it is the exact free Gaussian φ(y, t), which moves at the atom speed and spreads. Across the beam the spinor χ(x, z, t) is computed on a 128 × 128 grid with hard walls; the full wave is Ψ = φ(y, t) χ(x, z, t). The magnets act on χ through the field at the packet's centre, yc(t): a magnet turns on as the packet reaches it and off as it leaves, with smooth ramps 32 cells (0.3 cm) long. Lengths along the beam are drawn 2.5 times shorter than lengths across it, so the whole flight fits the view.

This treats the field as the same over the length of the packet. While the whole packet is inside a magnet, or outside every magnet, that is exact: the field does not change along y there, and the factorized wave is an exact solution. While the packet crosses a magnet's entrance or exit it is an approximation, for two reasons. Different parts of the packet move at slightly different speeds (a spread of about 1% here, the standard deviation of the speeds, set by the packet's length), so they spend slightly different times in the field; the factorized wave gives them all the same time. And the atom's magnetic energy changes as it enters the field; in a full calculation that energy would come from the motion along the beam and change the atom's speed, while here the speed stays fixed. The model atom's magnetic energy, (ħ/2m)|B|, is about 4% of its kinetic energy along the beam, so a full calculation would change its speed in the magnet by about 2%. For a silver atom in a 1 T field the magnetic energy is about 0.02% of the kinetic energy, and the packet is far shorter than the magnet's ends, so the same approximation is very good there: it is the one used in textbook treatments, where the atom's motion along the beam is classical. A separate full 3D calculation of the Pauli equation (one magnet, +z atom, screen at 2.8 cm) gives a deflection 2.3% smaller than this engine and a spot of the same width; the two spots differ by 2% of their weight. For an equal superposition the 3D upper and lower spots are deflected by amounts that differ by about 5%, because the atom gains speed in the field when its spin is along it and loses speed when it is against it. The Engine tab offers this full 3D calculation in the app.

In both engines the field at the magnet's ends is the field inside multiplied by the on/off profile p(y): B = p(y) B⊥(x, z). A real fringe field also has a small component along the beam, required by ∇×B = 0 where the field ramps up or down; this model leaves it out, so in the ramps its field is not curl-free. That component would tilt the field slightly in the ramps, which the packet crosses in about one and a half Larmor turns; its effect on the counts has not been computed here.

Full 3D engine

The Full 3D engine (Engine tab) solves the Pauli equation for the whole wave Ψ(x, y, z, t), with no factorization. To keep the grid small it works in the frame moving with the beam: Ψ = ei(p0y − E0t)/ħ Ψ′(y − yc(t), x, z), where yc = y0 + vt. This Galilean change of frame is exact; it removes the fast oscillation along the beam, so the grid only has to resolve the packet's envelope. Ψ′ lives on a window of 128 × 128 × 128 cells centred on yc(t); each cell feels the field at its own position along the beam, so the front of the packet enters the magnet before the back, and the atom gains or loses speed in the field according to its spin. The same fourth-order differences and leapfrog steps are used as across the beam in the fast engine, with hard walls on the faces of the window (the packet stays well inside it). The dots are guided by the same Pauli current, now with all three derivatives taken from Ψ′. The window takes about 64 MB of GPU memory (ψ′ at the current and the previous step) and each step costs about 128 times a step of the fast engine, so this engine runs much slower.

To keep these effects small the beam moves fast in grid units (44 cells per time unit) and the lengths along the beam are long in grid cells (the magnet is 400 cells). An earlier version used a speed eight times smaller, where the magnetic energy was 2.3 times the kinetic energy; a separate full 3D calculation then gave half the deflection for a +z atom. The flight time, the splitting and the lab units do not depend on this choice.

Magnetic field

For a magnet with its gradient along z the field is

B=g (−x′,0, 2+z′) , x′=x−xcL , z′=z−zcL

This is the form used by Griffiths and Schroeter (Introduction to Quantum Mechanics, 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2018), B = −αx x̂ + (B0 + αz) ẑ, with α = g/L and B0 = 2g. Here xc and zc are the centre of the box, L is half the box height, and g is the gradient strength, so ∂Bz/∂z = g/L. The uniform part, 2g, keeps the field from vanishing inside the box, so the spin components separate without spin flips (the Weak field setting uses 0.3g instead). The x component is required by ∇·B = 0; it has almost no effect on the probabilities here. A tilted magnet uses the same formula turned about the beam axis, and the field is multiplied by the magnet's on/off profile along the flight. The red lines are field lines of this B: at every point the line is tangent to B, and the arrowheads (Display: Field arrows) show which way B points along it. The lines carry no strength by their colour; the strength shows in their spacing, since the lines crowd together where |B| is larger, toward the top. That crowding is across x, so it is seen in the 3D and XZ views but not in the YZ view, where every line is seen edge-on as a vertical stroke. The Magnetic field switch (Display tab) turns the whole field off, uniform part included.

Field (Core). Textbook is the field above, whose gradient is the same everywhere. Knife edge treats the S pole as a sharp edge along the beam: far from the iron, the field of such an edge is that of a line pole, B = K(−x′, hp − z′)/(x′² + (hp − z′)²) in the magnet's frame, which has zero divergence and curl outside the line. Here the line sits 1.5 L above the centre (just above the tunnel) and K is chosen so that at the centre the gradient is the same as the textbook field's (the field there is 1.5 g instead of 2 g). The gradient then grows toward the edge (2.25 times larger halfway up, 0.56 times halfway down) and falls off to the sides (28% smaller halfway out), as under a real knife edge. The pole faces drawn for it are its equipotentials: circles about the line, a rounded edge and a groove. This is a model of the knife-edge field, not a calculation of the 1922 magnet. The Weak field setting keeps the textbook field.

Beam (Wave and guidance). Round is a circular packet. Ribbon is 2.5 times wider along x, like the long, narrow slit of 1922. With the knife-edge field the middle of the ribbon passes where the gradient is largest and is deflected most; its ends are deflected less, the upper part (toward the edge) much more so than the lower part. The two spots on the screen curve into the "lips" of the 1922 plate. A separate numpy calculation at the default gradient (5.1 in grid units) gives the upper spot 28.4 cells up in the middle and 16.2 cells at 40 cells to the side, and the lower spot 22.3 and 19.0 cells down. Because the lips bend toward the middle line at their ends, counting dots by halves of the screen no longer gives exactly 50/50 (47.5/52.5 in that calculation), although each spin component still carries half the probability. With the textbook field the spots stay straight.

The poles are drawn outside the tunnel over the part of the flight where each magnet is on. Their faces follow level curves of the magnetic potential of the chosen field, as iron pole faces do: gently curved faces for the textbook field, a rounded edge (S, blue) and a groove (N, red) for the knife-edge field. The field points from the N pole to the S pole and is stronger near the S pole. They are a picture of the source of the field; the computed field is the formula above, inside the tunnel. The screen's inner square is the tunnel's cross-section.

Wave

The particle is neutral, so there is no vector potential. The spinor obeys the Pauli equation

iℏ∂tΨ = - ℏ22m ∇2 - ℏ2m σ·B Ψ

With this sign the magnetic moment is parallel to the spin, μ = (ħ/2m)σ, and the +z component is pushed toward the stronger field, upward. For a silver atom the magnetic moment of the outer electron is antiparallel to its spin, so there the −z component would go up.

Across the beam the equation is solved with fourth-order finite differences and leapfrog steps in time. The initial packet is a Gaussian (Beam width) in all three directions, moving along y.

Guidance

The particle velocity is the Pauli current divided by the density. It has a convective part and a spin part. The convective part is

vconv = jconvρ
jconv = ℏm Im (Ψ†∇Ψ)

and the full velocity is

v = jconvρ + sℏ mρ ∇× (Ψ†σΨ)

Here s = ½: this is the Pauli current, the one that the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac current gives for g = 2 (P. Holland, Phys. Rev. A 60, 4326 (1999)). The spin term is a curl, so it has zero divergence and does not change how the density of dots evolves: with or without it the dots stay distributed as |Ψ|². It does change the individual trajectories; for example the dots of a +z packet circulate about the z axis, in the plane of the beam and x (seen in the XY view), at a rate of about ħ/(mσ²); only a spin along the beam would make them swirl about the beam axis. The Spin term switch (Advanced tab, Expert level) turns it off (s = 0, the convective current alone) to show that the statistics do not fix the trajectories. Where two parts of the wave still overlap at the screen, the counts on each side can differ slightly between the two choices, because the spin current carries dots across the dividing line; once the parts are well apart they agree. The controls use the lab units described below.

Units

The app shows lab units by giving each axis its own scale, matched to the 1922 experiment. This is possible because the motion along the beam (y) is computed separately from the motion across it (x and z). At the default settings the magnet is 3.5 cm long, the atoms move at 660 m/s, and the width of |Ψ|² across the beam (FWHM) is 35 µm, the width of the slit. The scales are then fixed, and the sliders change lab quantities: the field gradient in T/cm, the atom speed in m/s and the beam width in µm. The clock at the top left of the canvas gives the time since the packet started, in µs. The Axes button at the top right shows axes in these units: y in cm from the start of the tunnel, just behind the starting packet, to the screen, and x and z in µm from the middle of the box.

In 1922 the magnet was 3.5 cm long with a field gradient of about 10 T/cm, and the plate stood at the end of the magnet; the splitting was about 0.2 mm. Here the screen stands farther away, so a smaller gradient gives a similar splitting. The line under the controls gives the expected splitting from the classical estimate: each spin part is pushed with acceleration ±a·p(t), where a = (ħ/2m)(∂B/∂z)/m and p(t) is the magnet's on/off profile, so the splitting is 2a∫(T − t)p(t)dt with T the flight time to the screen.

What cannot match is ħ/m. In these units the model has ħ/m ≈ 5 × 10−6 m²/s, while for a silver atom ħ/m ≈ 6 × 10−10 m²/s, so the model's wave spreads about 8 × 103 times faster across the beam than a real silver atom's. That is the price of seeing the wave on a grid: the de Broglie wavelength of a silver atom at 660 m/s is about 6 × 10−12 m, so the real slit was millions of wavelengths wide, while here the packet is two to three grid wavelengths wide. A real silver beam hardly spreads at all in the magnet, which is why the real splitting looks so clean. Along the beam the grid wavelength has no lab meaning; there the motion only sets the clock. Internally the solver uses ħ = 6, m = 1 and a grid spacing of 1.

For instructors

A 50-minute class. (1) 5 min: what Stern and Gerlach saw in 1922 (two spots, not a smear); Basic level, default settings, press Start. (2) 10 min: predict, then watch: spin 0°, 90°, then 0° with the magnet at 60° (the sphere gives cos²(γ/2); compare with the counts and their scatter, about √(Np(1−p))). (3) 10 min: two magnets (Standard level): the four spots; then Magnet angle 90°, Second magnet 270° from the first, block the − part, spin +z: half of the passing atoms come back at −z. (4) 15 min: the two readings: switch View during a run; in Pilot Wave set Display: Dot colour to Starting half and compare Magnet angle 0° and 180° (contextuality); in Orthodox watch the detections appear and discuss collapse and the measurement problem. (5) 10 min: the Quiz tab, nine questions, answered before watching.

Points to flag. The model's magnetic moment is parallel to the spin, so +z goes toward the stronger field; for silver it is the other way. The counts are by nearest expected spot, so a few dots in a spot's tail can be counted with the other spot. The model's wave spreads about 8 × 10³ times faster than a silver atom's (see Units).
