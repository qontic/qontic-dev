Physics and model limits

What you see

The app opens in a 3D view; the XZ button gives the side view, with the beam moving to the right along x and z up. The cloud is the total density of the two-component spinor:

ρ(x,t) = Ψ†Ψ

The dots are pilot-wave particles. They start at random positions drawn from |Ψ|² and then follow the guidance law below. Yellow dots start on the + side of the packet along the first magnet's axis (above the centre for a z magnet), green dots on the other side. A dot stops when it reaches the detector plate, and the counts in the corner are made from those dots. The wave itself passes through the plate and keeps evolving.

The spin can point in any direction, set by two angles: θ from +z, and φ about z from the beam axis (+x) toward +y. The spinor is (cos θ/2, eiφ sin θ/2) in the ±z basis. The default, θ = 90° and φ = 0, points along the beam: an equal, in-phase superposition of +z and −z. In a z magnet the two spin components separate vertically. Each particle ends up in one branch, and which one is fixed by where it started: dots from the upper half of the packet go up, dots from the lower half go down. Only dots that start very near the middle can end in the other branch.

Magnets

The Magnets control chooses the field along the flight:

  • Long magnet: a z magnet all the way to the plate.
  • Short magnet, then drift: the field acts over the first third of the flight; after it the two parts keep the sideways speed they gained and drift apart, as after the real 3.5 cm magnet.
  • Two magnets at 90°: a short magnet, a field-free gap, then a second magnet turned 90° from the first (at Magnet angle 0: a z magnet, then a y magnet). A +z packet first moves up as a whole, then splits into +y and −y. The equal superposition ends in four spots.
  • Weak uniform field: a long z magnet whose uniform part is small, so the field nearly vanishes near the bottom of the box. There the spin cannot follow the field and flips; the lower spot is distorted and the counts are no longer 50/50.

Each setting has its own default and largest gradient, chosen to keep the parts away from the walls of the box.

The Magnet angle turns every magnet about the beam axis, from +z toward +y: 90° measures spin along +y, 180° along −z. The magnet can only turn about the beam: the beam has to pass between the poles, and a gradient along the beam would speed the atoms up or slow them down without splitting them. A magnet whose axis makes an angle γ with the spin sends the fraction cos²(γ/2) to its + side; the sphere next to the controls shows γ and this prediction. For example, a +z spin in a magnet turned 45° gives cos² 22.5° ≈ 85% and 15%.

Two readings

The interpretation button in the controls switches between two readings of the same wave. In the pilot-wave reading the particle has a position at all times and is guided by the wave; the dots show possible positions. In the orthodox reading only the wave is shown: the theory gives the particle no position before it is measured, and |Ψ|² gives the probabilities of where it will be found at the plate. For the equal superposition each half of the plate gets probability ½; for +z the upper half gets nearly all of it. Both readings predict the same statistics at the plate. In the orthodox reading the box in the corner shows the weight of |Ψ|² in each region of the plate (two halves along the magnet's axis, or four quadrants after two magnets); when the packet reaches the plate, these are the chances of a hit there.

Geometry

The canvas shows one tunnel from just behind the starting packet (x = 0) to the detector plate. The wave is computed in two parts. Along the beam it is the exact free Gaussian φ(x, t), which moves at the atom speed and spreads. Across the beam the spinor χ(y, z, t) is computed on a 128 × 128 grid with hard walls; the full wave is Ψ = φ(x, t) χ(y, z, t). The magnets act on χ through the field at the packet's centre, xc(t): a magnet turns on as the packet reaches it and off as it leaves, with smooth ramps over 5% of the flight.

This treats the field as the same over the length of the packet. At the magnet ends that is a simplification: the packet (about 0.5 cm long in lab units) is longer than the ramps (about 0.2 cm), and real fringe fields also have a component along the beam. Inside a magnet, where the field does not change along x, the factorization is exact.

Magnetic field

For a magnet with its gradient along z the field is

B=g (0,−y′, 2+z′) , y′=y−ycL , z′=z−zcL

Here yc and zc are the centre of the box, L is half the box height, and g is the gradient strength, so ∂Bz/∂z = g/L. The uniform part, 2g, keeps the field from vanishing inside the box, so the spin components separate without spin flips (the Weak uniform field setting uses 0.3g instead). The y component is required by ∇·B = 0; it has almost no effect on the probabilities here. A tilted magnet uses the same formula turned about the beam axis, and the field is multiplied by the magnet's on/off profile along the flight. The red lines are field lines of this B: at every point the line is tangent to B, and the arrowheads (Display: Field arrows) show which way B points along it. The lines carry no strength by their colour; the strength shows in their spacing, since the lines crowd together where |B| is larger, toward the top. That crowding is across y, so it is seen in the 3D and YZ views but not in the XZ view, where every line is seen edge-on as a vertical stroke. The Magnetic field switch (Display tab) turns the whole field off, uniform part included.

The poles are drawn outside the tunnel over the part of the flight where each magnet is on. The field points from the N pole (red, flat with a shallow groove) to the S pole (blue, a knife edge) and is stronger near the knife edge, as in the 1922 magnet. They are a picture of the source of the field; the computed field is the formula above, inside the tunnel. The screen at the end is the detector plate; its inner square is the tunnel's cross-section.

Wave

The particle is neutral, so there is no vector potential. The spinor obeys the Pauli equation

iℏ∂tΨ = - ℏ22m ∇2 - ℏ2m σ·B Ψ

With this sign the magnetic moment is parallel to the spin, μ = (ħ/2m)σ, and the +z component is pushed toward the stronger field, upward. For a silver atom the magnetic moment of the outer electron is antiparallel to its spin, so there the −z component would go up.

Across the beam the equation is solved with fourth-order finite differences and leapfrog steps in time. The initial packet is a Gaussian (Beam width) in all three directions, moving along x.

Guidance

The particle velocity is the Pauli current divided by the density. It has a convective part and a spin part. The convective part is

vconv = jconvρ
jconv = ℏm Im (Ψ†∇Ψ)

and the full velocity is

v = jconvρ + sℏ mρ ∇× (Ψ†σΨ)

Here s = ½: this is the Pauli current, the one that the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac current gives for g = 2. The spin term is a curl, so it has zero divergence and does not change how the density of dots evolves: with or without it the dots stay distributed as |Ψ|². It does change the individual trajectories; for example the dots of a +z packet swirl around the beam axis. The Spin term switch in the Advanced tab turns it off (s = 0, the convective current alone) to show that the statistics do not fix the trajectories. Where two parts of the wave still overlap at the plate, the counts on each side can differ slightly between the two choices, because the spin current carries dots across the dividing line; once the parts are well apart they agree. The controls use the lab units described below.

Units

The app shows lab units by giving each axis its own scale, matched to the 1922 experiment. This is possible because the motion along the beam (x) is computed separately from the motion across it (y and z). At the default settings the flight from the start to the plate is the 3.5 cm magnet, the atoms move at 660 m/s, and the width of |Ψ|² across the beam (FWHM) is 35 µm, the width of the slit. The scales are then fixed, and the sliders change lab quantities: the field gradient in T/cm, the atom speed in m/s and the beam width in µm. The clock at the top left of the canvas gives the time since the packet started, in µs. The Axes button at the top right shows axes in these units: x in cm from the start of the tunnel, just behind the starting packet, to the plate, and y and z in µm from the middle of the box.

The gradient and the splitting come out close to the 1922 values: the magnet was 3.5 cm long with a field gradient of about 10 T/cm, and the splitting at the plate was about 0.2 mm. The line under the controls gives the expected splitting from the classical estimate: each spin part is pushed with acceleration ±a·p(t), where a = (ħ/2m)(∂B/∂z)/m and p(t) is the magnet's on/off profile, so for the long magnet the splitting is a·T² with T the flight time.

What cannot match is ħ/m. In these units the model has ħ/m ≈ 10−5 m²/s, while for a silver atom ħ/m ≈ 6 × 10−10 m²/s, so the model's wave spreads about 2 × 104 times faster across the beam than a real silver atom's. That is the price of seeing the wave on a grid: the de Broglie wavelength of a silver atom at 660 m/s is about 6 × 10−12 m, so the real slit was millions of wavelengths wide, while here the packet is two to three grid wavelengths wide. A real silver beam hardly spreads at all in the magnet, which is why the real splitting looks so clean. Along the beam the grid wavelength has no lab meaning; there the motion only sets the clock. Internally the solver uses ħ = 6, m = 1 and a grid spacing of 1.
