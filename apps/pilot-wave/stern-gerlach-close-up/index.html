Physics and model limits

What you see

The axes match the textbook field of Griffiths and Schroeter (below), which depends on x and z only: the beam travels along y, z is up, and x points across the beam (x, y, z right-handed). The app opens in a 3D view; the YZ button gives the side view, with the beam moving to the right along y and z up, and XZ looks along the beam. The cloud is the total density of the two-component spinor:

ρ(x,t) = Ψ†Ψ

The dots are pilot-wave particles. They start at random positions drawn from |Ψ|² and then follow the guidance law below. Yellow dots start on the + side of the packet along the first magnet's axis (above the centre for a z magnet), green dots on the other side. A dot stops when it reaches the screen, and the counts in the corner are made from those dots. The wave itself passes through the screen and keeps evolving.

The spin can point in any direction, set by two angles: θ from +z, and φ about z from +x toward +y (the beam). The spinor is (cos θ/2, eiφ sin θ/2) in the ±z basis. The default, θ = 90° and φ = 0, points along +x, across the beam: an equal, in-phase superposition of +z and −z. In a z magnet the two spin components separate vertically. Each particle ends up in one branch, and which one is fixed by where it started: dots from the upper half of the packet go up, dots from the lower half go down. Only dots that start very near the middle can end in the other branch.

Display: Show phase colours the cloud by the phase of the wave. The fast oscillation along the beam, ei(kx − ωt), is left out, because it is shorter than the spacing of the drawn points; what is shown is the phase relative to the beam's plane wave.

Magnets

The packet starts in a field-free region, 2.1 cm before the magnet, passes the 3.5 cm magnet, and drifts to the screen. Screen distance sets how far the screen stands after the end of the last magnet (0.7 to 4.2 cm); after the magnet the two parts keep the sideways speed they gained, so a farther screen shows a larger splitting. The Magnets control chooses the field:

  • One magnet: a single magnet, 3.5 cm long.
  • Two magnets at 90°: a short magnet, a field-free gap, then a second magnet turned 90° from the first (at Magnet angle 0: a z magnet, then an x magnet). A +z packet first moves up as a whole, then splits into +x and −x. The equal superposition ends in four spots. Off the axis the second magnet's field is slightly tilted, so its split is not exactly 50/50.
  • Weak field: one magnet whose uniform part is small, so the field nearly vanishes on one side of the box. There the spin cannot follow the field and flips; one spot is distorted and the counts are no longer 50/50.

Edit geometry (the pencil in the toolbar, or right-click the canvas) shows handles on the canvas: drag them to move the magnet's start and end and the screen along the beam, or turn the magnet about the beam. You can also drag on the small sphere next to the spin sliders to point the spin. Each change restarts the packet; Reset geometry returns to the 1922-like layout.

The gradient slider stops where the parts would reach the walls of the box, out to 2.5 standard deviations of |Ψ|². The limit depends on the magnets and the screen distance: the wave spreads as it travels, so a farther screen allows a smaller gradient. Choosing a magnet setting sets the gradient to 90% of the limit; moving the screen lowers the gradient only if it is above the new limit. When the expected splitting is less than four standard deviations, the line under the controls says that the spots overlap.

The Magnet angle turns every magnet about the beam axis, from +z toward +x: 90° measures spin along +x, 180° along −z. The magnet can only turn about the beam: the beam has to pass between the poles, and a gradient along the beam would speed the atoms up or slow them down without splitting them. A magnet whose axis makes an angle γ with the spin sends the fraction cos²(γ/2) to its + side; the sphere next to the controls shows γ and this prediction. For example, a +z spin in a magnet turned 45° gives cos² 22.5° ≈ 85% and 15%.

Spin arrows

Display: Spin arrow draws the spin ⟨σ⟩ of the whole state at the packet (described below), in both readings; Spin on dots adds, in the pilot-wave reading, a small arrow on each dot. Each small arrow sits on a dot and points along the spin direction of the wave at the dot's position, Ψ†σΨ/Ψ†Ψ. For a single particle this is a unit vector at every point, so the arrows always have full length. Before the magnet all arrows point along the prepared spin; once the parts separate, the arrows in each part point along + or − the magnet axis, and dots in the region where the parts still overlap show intermediate directions. In the minimal version of pilot-wave theory, presented for example by Norsen, only the position is a property of the particle, so the arrow is a property of the wave at the particle; Dewdney, Holland and Kyprianidis instead treat it as the particle's spin, carried along its trajectory.

T. Norsen, “The pilot-wave perspective on spin,” Am. J. Phys. 82, 337–348 (2014), arXiv:1305.1280; C. Dewdney, P. R. Holland and A. Kyprianidis, “What happens in a spin measurement?,” Phys. Lett. A 119, 259–267 (1986).

In the orthodox reading there is one arrow, at the centre of |Ψ|²: the expectation value ⟨σ⟩ of the whole state. Its length is 1 while the spin has a definite direction, that is, while the state is an eigenstate of some spin component. As the two parts separate, spin and position become entangled and the arrow shrinks; for an equal split it goes to zero: the spin alone then has no definite direction, only the probabilities of the two outcomes. Each part on its own has a definite spin along the magnet axis.

Inside the magnet the arrow turns about the field direction: the part of the spin across B precesses at the Larmor frequency, while the part along B stays fixed. For the default +x spin in a z magnet the arrow therefore spins around the z axis in the x–y plane, and shrinks as the parts separate. In the model one turn takes about 3 µs on the clock (much faster for a real silver atom: about 0.4 ns in a 0.1 T field). The arrow is redrawn a few times per second, so its direction appears to jump. A spin along the magnet axis does not precess.

Two readings

The interpretation button in the controls switches between two readings of the same wave. In the pilot-wave reading the particle has a position at all times and is guided by the wave; the dots show possible positions. In the orthodox reading only the wave is shown: the theory gives the particle no position before it is measured, and |Ψ|² gives the probabilities of where it will be found at the screen. For the equal superposition each half of the screen gets probability ½; for +z the upper half gets nearly all of it. Both readings predict the same statistics at the screen. In the orthodox reading the box in the corner shows the weight of |Ψ|² in each region of the screen (two halves along the magnet's axis, or four quadrants after two magnets); when the packet reaches the screen, these are the chances of a hit there.

Geometry

The canvas shows one tunnel from just behind the starting packet (x = 0) to the screen. The wave is computed in two parts. Along the beam it is the exact free Gaussian φ(y, t), which moves at the atom speed and spreads. Across the beam the spinor χ(x, z, t) is computed on a 128 × 128 grid with hard walls; the full wave is Ψ = φ(y, t) χ(x, z, t). The magnets act on χ through the field at the packet's centre, yc(t): a magnet turns on as the packet reaches it and off as it leaves, with smooth ramps 32 cells (0.3 cm) long. Lengths along the beam are drawn 2.5 times shorter than lengths across it, so the whole flight fits the view.

This treats the field as the same over the length of the packet. While the whole packet is inside a magnet, or outside every magnet, that is exact: the field does not change along y there, and the factorized wave is an exact solution. While the packet crosses a magnet's entrance or exit it is an approximation, for two reasons. Different parts of the packet move at slightly different speeds (a spread of about 1.4% here, set by the packet's length), so they spend slightly different times in the field; the factorized wave gives them all the same time. And the atom's magnetic energy changes as it enters the field; in a full calculation that energy would come from the motion along the beam and change the atom's speed, while here the speed stays fixed. The model atom's magnetic energy, (ħ/2m)|B|, is about 4% of its kinetic energy along the beam, so a full calculation would change its speed in the magnet by about 2%. For a silver atom in a 1 T field the magnetic energy is about 0.02% of the kinetic energy, and the packet is far shorter than the magnet's ends, so the same approximation is very good there: it is the one used in textbook treatments, where the atom's motion along the beam is classical. A separate full 3D calculation of the Pauli equation (one magnet, +z atom, screen at 2.8 cm) gives a deflection 2.3% smaller than this engine and a spot of the same width; the two spots differ by 2% of their weight. For an equal superposition the 3D upper and lower spots are deflected by amounts that differ by about 5%, because the atom gains speed in the field when its spin is along it and loses speed when it is against it. The Engine tab offers this full 3D calculation in the app.

Full 3D engine

The Full 3D engine (Engine tab) solves the Pauli equation for the whole wave Ψ(x, y, z, t), with no factorization. To keep the grid small it works in the frame moving with the beam: Ψ = ei(p0y − E0t)/ħ Ψ′(y − yc(t), x, z), where yc = y0 + vt. This Galilean change of frame is exact; it removes the fast oscillation along the beam, so the grid only has to resolve the packet's envelope. Ψ′ lives on a window of 128 × 128 × 128 cells centred on yc(t); each cell feels the field at its own position along the beam, so the front of the packet enters the magnet before the back, and the atom gains or loses speed in the field according to its spin. The same fourth-order differences and leapfrog steps are used as across the beam in the fast engine, with hard walls on the faces of the window (the packet stays well inside it). The dots are guided by the same Pauli current, now with all three derivatives taken from Ψ′. The window takes about 130 MB of GPU memory and each step costs about 128 times a step of the fast engine, so this engine runs much slower.

To keep these effects small the beam moves fast in grid units (44 cells per time unit) and the lengths along the beam are long in grid cells (the magnet is 400 cells). An earlier version used a speed eight times smaller, where the magnetic energy was 2.3 times the kinetic energy; a separate full 3D calculation then gave half the deflection for a +z atom. The flight time, the splitting and the lab units do not depend on this choice.

Magnetic field

For a magnet with its gradient along z the field is

B=g (−x′,0, 2+z′) , x′=x−xcL , z′=z−zcL

This is the form used by Griffiths and Schroeter (Introduction to Quantum Mechanics, 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2018), B = −αx x̂ + (B0 + αz) ẑ, with α = g/L and B0 = 2g. Here xc and zc are the centre of the box, L is half the box height, and g is the gradient strength, so ∂Bz/∂z = g/L. The uniform part, 2g, keeps the field from vanishing inside the box, so the spin components separate without spin flips (the Weak field setting uses 0.3g instead). The x component is required by ∇·B = 0; it has almost no effect on the probabilities here. A tilted magnet uses the same formula turned about the beam axis, and the field is multiplied by the magnet's on/off profile along the flight. The red lines are field lines of this B: at every point the line is tangent to B, and the arrowheads (Display: Field arrows) show which way B points along it. The lines carry no strength by their colour; the strength shows in their spacing, since the lines crowd together where |B| is larger, toward the top. That crowding is across x, so it is seen in the 3D and XZ views but not in the YZ view, where every line is seen edge-on as a vertical stroke. The Magnetic field switch (Display tab) turns the whole field off, uniform part included.

The poles are drawn outside the tunnel over the part of the flight where each magnet is on. The field points from the N pole (red, flat with a shallow groove) to the S pole (blue, a knife edge) and is stronger near the knife edge, as in the 1922 magnet. They are a picture of the source of the field; the computed field is the formula above, inside the tunnel. The screen's inner square is the tunnel's cross-section.

Wave

The particle is neutral, so there is no vector potential. The spinor obeys the Pauli equation

iℏ∂tΨ = - ℏ22m ∇2 - ℏ2m σ·B Ψ

With this sign the magnetic moment is parallel to the spin, μ = (ħ/2m)σ, and the +z component is pushed toward the stronger field, upward. For a silver atom the magnetic moment of the outer electron is antiparallel to its spin, so there the −z component would go up.

Across the beam the equation is solved with fourth-order finite differences and leapfrog steps in time. The initial packet is a Gaussian (Beam width) in all three directions, moving along y.

Guidance

The particle velocity is the Pauli current divided by the density. It has a convective part and a spin part. The convective part is

vconv = jconvρ
jconv = ℏm Im (Ψ†∇Ψ)

and the full velocity is

v = jconvρ + sℏ mρ ∇× (Ψ†σΨ)

Here s = ½: this is the Pauli current, the one that the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac current gives for g = 2. The spin term is a curl, so it has zero divergence and does not change how the density of dots evolves: with or without it the dots stay distributed as |Ψ|². It does change the individual trajectories; for example the dots of a +z packet swirl around the beam axis. The Spin term switch in the Advanced tab turns it off (s = 0, the convective current alone) to show that the statistics do not fix the trajectories. Where two parts of the wave still overlap at the screen, the counts on each side can differ slightly between the two choices, because the spin current carries dots across the dividing line; once the parts are well apart they agree. The controls use the lab units described below.

Units

The app shows lab units by giving each axis its own scale, matched to the 1922 experiment. This is possible because the motion along the beam (y) is computed separately from the motion across it (x and z). At the default settings the magnet is 3.5 cm long, the atoms move at 660 m/s, and the width of |Ψ|² across the beam (FWHM) is 35 µm, the width of the slit. The scales are then fixed, and the sliders change lab quantities: the field gradient in T/cm, the atom speed in m/s and the beam width in µm. The clock at the top left of the canvas gives the time since the packet started, in µs. The Axes button at the top right shows axes in these units: y in cm from the start of the tunnel, just behind the starting packet, to the screen, and x and z in µm from the middle of the box.

In 1922 the magnet was 3.5 cm long with a field gradient of about 10 T/cm, and the plate stood at the end of the magnet; the splitting was about 0.2 mm. Here the screen stands farther away, so a smaller gradient gives a similar splitting. The line under the controls gives the expected splitting from the classical estimate: each spin part is pushed with acceleration ±a·p(t), where a = (ħ/2m)(∂B/∂z)/m and p(t) is the magnet's on/off profile, so the splitting is 2a∫(T − t)p(t)dt with T the flight time to the screen.

What cannot match is ħ/m. In these units the model has ħ/m ≈ 5 × 10−6 m²/s, while for a silver atom ħ/m ≈ 6 × 10−10 m²/s, so the model's wave spreads about 8 × 103 times faster across the beam than a real silver atom's. That is the price of seeing the wave on a grid: the de Broglie wavelength of a silver atom at 660 m/s is about 6 × 10−12 m, so the real slit was millions of wavelengths wide, while here the packet is two to three grid wavelengths wide. A real silver beam hardly spreads at all in the magnet, which is why the real splitting looks so clean. Along the beam the grid wavelength has no lab meaning; there the motion only sets the clock. Internally the solver uses ħ = 6, m = 1 and a grid spacing of 1.
