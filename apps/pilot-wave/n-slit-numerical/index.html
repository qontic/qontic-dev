Theory

Which slit: successive single particles start from positions biased toward the openings. After a particle passes a slit, detection farther downstream smoothly prepares a broad Gaussian centered on that slit. Its width allows the particle to continue more gently while keeping the new wave clear of the wall. The particle keeps its position and follows the spreading wave. Actual detector hits accumulate across particles. This is an illustrative preparation with biased launches; its histogram is not an exact Born-rule prediction.

A quantum wave packet travels according to the Schrödinger equation, solved here numerically on a grid (leapfrog in time, five-point Laplacian; units with ħ = 6, m = 1, grid step 1, so the default momentum p = 1.5 gives λ = 2πħ/p ≈ 25 grid steps). The wall splits it into coherent parts, one per open slit; after the slits those parts overlap and form interference. There are 1–16 slits, slit 1 at the top; a click cycles a slit through open, closed and detector. The packet's width across the motion follows the slit span, max(σ, (N − 1) × separation / 2), so all slits are lit; it is kept clear of the absorbing edges.

Detectors at the slits

With detectors at slits D the state behind the wall is Σk∈D ψk|dk⟩ + (Σj∉D ψj)|d0⟩: one record per detected slit and one for "no detector fired", in which the undetected open slits still interfere. Records are orthogonal, so the density behind the wall is Σk∈D|ψk|² + |Σj∉D ψj|². The detectors are ideal: they record the slit and do not disturb the wave.

How it is computed: the equation is linear, and the wave behind the wall is set by its values on the wall's exit face. Each record has its own field behind the wall, driven on the exit face by the total wave times a smooth weight that shares the face among the open slits (the weights add to 1). The records' fields add up to the total wave behind the wall (checked: to 1.5×10⁻⁴), and the picture shows them added. A Pilot-Wave particle is guided by the total wave until it leaves the wall; its slit then fixes its record, and from then on it is guided by that record's wave (the other records are empty waves). In Orthodox and Many-Worlds terms the record is the measured slit or the branch; this app shows the pilot-wave account.

Absorbing wall

With the wall set to absorbing, particles that hit it are absorbed instead of turned back. The wall is then 20 grid steps thick: an imaginary potential −iW rising quadratically to W = 3 over its front 90%, and the wall potential behind it. A thin absorber reflects much of a wave; in a one-dimensional test with the default packet this design reflects 0.7% and transmits 2×10⁻⁵ (a 10-step absorbing wall reflected at least 6%). Pilot-Wave particles in the absorber are removed with the probability that |ψ|² is absorbed there (effective rule).

Closing a slit during a run

A slit clicked during a run is closed (or opened) by a blade that slides across it from its upper edge over the closing time τ. A reflecting blade is wall: the potential V₀ = 50 (the packet's energy is about 1.1) fills the covered part. An absorbing blade is an imaginary potential −iW in the covered part, which removes |ψ|² at the rate 2W/ħ; Pilot-Wave particles there are removed with the same probability (an effective rule, not a model of the absorber). With wait for packet on, the blade starts when the packet centre reaches the wall.

Show change. At the first slit click of a run the app copies the wave into a second simulation in which the slits stay as they were. The picture can then show ψ − ψref, the change caused by the shutter, and the stats give |δψ|/|ψ| (root-mean-square, relative) ahead of and behind the cut: the slice that left the wall when the blade started, moving on at v = p/m.

What closing a slit changes

The equation is linear, so the wave at a later time is the sum of what evolves from the part that had already passed the wall and from the part that had not. The part already past hardly overlaps the slit any more, so closing the slit changes it very little. In a reference calculation with this app's geometry (split-operator method on a finer grid, slit 2 closed by a reflecting blade when the packet centre reaches the wall) that part changed by 0.7% of its amplitude (0.2% ahead of the cut).

The part still in front of the wall or inside the slit is changed, and it does not stay behind the cut. Its faster components (from the packet's spread of speeds, and the faster waves scattered when the blade closes suddenly) move into the region the earlier part occupies. Diffraction to wide angles slows the forward motion, so it leaves wave behind the cut, not ahead of it. So the total wave ahead of the cut changes, and so does the motion of the Pilot-Wave particles there. In the reference calculation, with the default packet (packet sigma = 25 ≈ λ; this is the amplitude width, the density width is 25/√2 ≈ 0.7λ), the later part made up 83% of the amplitude ahead of the cut even with the slit left open (with a smooth split that counts some wave just past the wall as later; counting only wave still behind the wall exit gives about 65%); closing the slit changed the wave ahead of the cut by 52% (instantaneous reflecting blade), 44% (instantaneous absorbing blade) and 24% (reflecting blade over τ = 60), all of it coming from the later part. A longer packet (packet sigma = 60, density width 1.7λ) lowers the later part's share to 54% and the change ahead of the cut, for the instantaneous reflecting blade, to 33%; the part already past still changes by less than 0.3%.

The analytical N-slit app uses the paraxial approximation, which carries each slice of the packet forward at v and has no spread of forward speeds; there the later part cannot reach the region ahead of the cut, and closing a slit changes nothing there. That is the limit of a long packet, small diffraction angles and a closure that scatters little.

Numerical notes: the grid is 560 steps wide at the default sim scale on every screen (its height follows the canvas shape), and the picture is scaled to fit. The time step is constant: the speed control changes the number of steps per frame, because leapfrog needs a fixed step, and the step is kept below 0.9 of its stability limit 1/(4ħ/m + V₀/ħ). A weak Robert–Asselin filter (ν = 0.01) removes leapfrog's spurious alternating mode, which the high wall otherwise traps and shows as a glow; its effect on travelling waves is about 10⁻⁷ per step. The wall, the blade and the absorbing boundaries have smooth edges one grid step wide. The app and the reference calculation agree in order of magnitude; the app's grid and time step are coarser. With repeat (↻) on, each run restarts from the slits as they were at its start, replays the slit clicks at the same times, and adds its hits to the histogram.

A Bohmian particle has a definite position, and its velocity is determined by the probability current. The guiding law is:

ẋ = ∇Sm = jρ,    j = ℏm Im(ψ*∇ψ)

The Pauli option adds an extra term in the current arising from the spin. Paths can change while the same wave density still governs the pattern.
