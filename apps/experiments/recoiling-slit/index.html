The joint state of light and atom

A photon scattered by the atom is collected either upward (path A) or downward (path B). By momentum conservation the atom recoils by −ħk or +ħk along the same axis. In position space a kick multiplies the atom’s wave by e∓ikx. Just after scattering the photon and atom share one state:

Ψ = [ fA e−ikxψ₀(x) + eiφ fB e+ikxψ₀(x) ] / √2,

where ψ₀ is the atom’s ground state in the tweezer and fA, fB are the photon modes. In momentum space the two atom waves are the ground state shifted to p = −ħk and p = +ħk. The scattering lasts nanoseconds, far shorter than the trap period (25–110 µs across the measured depths), so the atom does not move appreciably during one event.

Trap depth sets the momentum spread

In a harmonic trap of frequency ω the ground state has Δp = (ħmω/2)½ and Δx = ħ/(2Δp). The trap frequency grows as the square root of the depth U, so Δp ∝ U¼. We anchor this at the paper’s Δp = 1.60 ħk for U = 10.49 mK; it then gives 0.78 ħk at 0.60 mK, the paper’s other end point. For rubidium-87 at λ = 780.24 nm, 1.60 ħk corresponds to an axial trap frequency of 38.6 kHz; the paper quotes about 40 kHz. The wavelength is our inference: the paper names the F = 2 → F′ = 3 cycling transition, which is the D2 line.

Recombination and visibility

The beam splitter adds and subtracts the two paths. Each detector is paired with an atom wave:

χ±(p) = [ ψ₀(p + ħk) ± eiφ ψ₀(p − ħk) ] / 2,
P(D±) = ∫|χ±|² dp = ½[1 ± V cos φ],
V = |⟨ψ₀(p + ħk)|ψ₀(p − ħk)⟩| = e−2η², η = ħk / (2Δp).

Zhang et al. give the same value, e−2η². The visibility is the modulus of the overlap, not its square. The fringes fade when the two recoil waves can be told apart, that is, when the atom carries path information. No one has to read the atom for this to happen.

The same result in position space

For an atom at position x the two paths reach D± with relative phase 2kx + φ:

P(D± | x) = ½[1 ± cos(2kx + φ)].

This pattern has period λ/2 ≈ 390 nm. Averaging it over |ψ₀(x)|², a Gaussian of width Δx, gives the same V = e−2k²Δx² = e−2η². Across the measured range Δx is 39–80 nm. A tightly held atom samples nearly one phase and the fringes survive; a loosely held one samples many phases and they wash out. The pilot-wave view uses this picture.

Heating

With n̄ thermal phonons the visibility becomes V = e−2η²(2n̄+1), as in the paper. This looks paradoxical: heating widens the momentum distribution, which by the overlap rule should help. But the overlap rule is for a pure state. A thermal state is a mixture of ground states displaced in position and momentum (coherent states). Each one has the same overlap of its two recoil waves, but each sees the stripe pattern shifted by its displacement, so their fringes are out of step and average down. In position terms: the atom is spread over more of the 390 nm stripes. The app samples exactly this mixture; the wave plots show the pure ground-state waves.

Comparison with the experiment

The paper gives its visibilities as plots (Fig. 3a): raw values close to the ideal curve and slightly below it, in agreement once the measured n̄ = 0.08–0.37 at each point is included. The text quotes one number: 84(4)% at 10.49 mK, at the start of a time-resolved run (Fig. 4a). The model gives 82.3% for the ground state and 79.7% with n̄ = 0.08. The visibility graph shows the ground-state curve, the band for the measured n̄ range, and this point.

What detection changes in the display

Before detection all three views use the same wave calculation. After detection the Orthodox display keeps the recorded χ and normalizes it. Pilot Wave keeps both components and shows the atom’s actual position and one definite record. Many Worlds keeps both record branches with their Born weights. Every view samples counts from the same probabilities.

The optional atom readout

A later momentum reading is drawn from the momentum density paired with the recorded detector. Quantum mechanics gives no joint distribution of the atom’s position and a later momentum reading, so the app does not relate the two.

Limits of this model

One dimension along the recoil axis; a harmonic trap; a balanced, lossless two-mode interferometer. Exact within the model: the waves, the detector probabilities, the visibility and the sampled statistics. Schematic: the drawn light packet, its enlarged wavelength and its timing. Omitted: motion in the trap during and after the event, the build-up of heating during continuous scattering (Fig. 4 of the paper), optical losses, background counts and detector noise. The measured phase noise, 16.5 mrad rms, would lower V by less than 0.02%.

Orthodox

Before detection the photon–atom state contains both correlated alternatives. Detection selects one recorded outcome; the atom wave paired with it is normalized and the other component is removed. The fringes fade before any collapse, because the atom’s recoil waves become distinguishable. This is Bohr’s complementarity: an apparatus that could record the path cannot also show the fringe. The atom does not need a position or momentum value between measurements; the stripe curve in Part 1 gives the joint odds of a click and a later position reading.

Pilot wave

Here the atom always has a definite position X, and its wave guides it. In the ground state the wave is real, so the atom sits still; across many runs X is distributed as |ψ₀|² (quantum equilibrium). The kicks change the phase of the atom’s wave, e∓ikx, but not where the atom is during the brief scattering.

For an atom at X the wave gives detector D₊ with probability ½[1 + cos(2kX + φ)]. So the slit has a definite position, as Einstein assumed, and that position sets the odds of each outcome. The fringes fade because X varies from run to run over a range comparable with λ/2, and nobody can know X better than |ψ₀|² allows. This is where Bohr’s uncertainty relation enters. The pilot-wave panel shows X, this probability curve, and the positions behind the D₊ and D₋ clicks once you collect events.

Which detector fires in a given run also depends on the configuration of the light field and the detectors. Photons need a field or particle formulation of pilot-wave theory, and the app does not model it: it draws the record from P(D± | X). The correlation between the atom’s position and the detector is a quantum prediction shared by all three views; the pilot-wave view reads it as a fact about where the atom was.

W. Struyve and H. Westman, “A minimalist pilot-wave model for quantum electrodynamics,” Proc. R. Soc. A 463, 3115–3129 (2007). An example of a field formulation for light.

Many worlds

The quantum state continues without collapse. Detection correlates it with separate macroscopic records, D₊ and D₋. Both nonzero-weight branches remain on screen; within either, an observer sees one outcome. The branch weights are the squared amplitudes.

The colored recoil components before detection are not yet separate worlds. Momentum separation alone is not decoherence; the record panels stand for the later detector records.

The experiment tests the shared quantum prediction. It does not choose between these views.

The 1927 argument

At the 1927 Solvay conference Einstein proposed a double slit whose first slit can move. The recoil of that slit would reveal which way each particle went, while the screen still shows fringes. Bohr replied that a slit light enough to register the recoil must have a momentum uncertainty comparable with the kick, so its position is uncertain over a fringe spacing and the pattern washes out.

The experiment

Zhang et al. used a single rubidium-87 atom in an optical tweezer as the movable slit.

  1. Prepare. Cool the atom to the motional ground state in all three directions (axial ground-state occupation 0.91).
  2. Scatter. Scatter near-resonant light on a closed cycling transition. Collect photons emitted up and down; the atom recoils oppositely.
  3. Recombine. Bring the two collection paths together on a beam splitter, with the phase locked to 16.5 mrad rms.
  4. Detect. Count single photons at the two outputs while scanning the phase, and fit the fringe.

Changing the tweezer’s trap depth from 0.60 mK to 10.49 mK tuned the atom’s momentum spread from 0.78 ħk to 1.60 ħk. A shallower trap, a more “movable” slit, gave lower visibility. Measuring the residual phonon number separated this quantum effect from classical heating.

Y.-C. Zhang, H.-W. Cheng, Z.-Q. Zengxu, Z. Wu, R. Lin, Y.-C. Duan, J. Rui, M.-C. Chen, C.-Y. Lu and J.-W. Pan, “Tunable Einstein–Bohr recoiling-slit gedankenexperiment at the quantum limit,” Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 230202 (2025). Preprint: arXiv:2410.10664.

What it shows, and what it does not

The loss of visibility follows from the overlap of the atom’s two recoil states, at the quantum limit, without noise from the environment. Bohr’s conclusion holds. The authors stress that the loss of interference comes from entanglement between the photon and the atom’s motion, not from classical noise. The experiment confirms the standard quantum prediction; all three views on the Views tab agree with it.
