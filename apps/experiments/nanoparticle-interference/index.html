    The experiment

    In 2026 the group of Markus Arndt and Stefan Gerlich in Vienna, with Klaus Hornberger in Duisburg-Essen, reported interference of sodium clusters with masses above 170,000 Da, more than 7,000 atoms each.1 Sodium vapour condenses into clusters in a cold argon–helium gas at about 77 K. The clusters fly at about 160 m/s through three gratings and are then ionised by a 425 nm laser and counted in a quadrupole mass spectrometer, which picks one mass window. Their de Broglie wavelength λdB = h/mv is 10–22 fm, about a million times smaller than the cluster.

    Why three gratings: Talbot–Lau interferometry

    A wave passing a grating of period d recreates an image of the grating at multiples of the Talbot length LT = d²/λdB. This self-imaging was first seen with light by Talbot in 1836.2 A Talbot–Lau interferometer uses it with a beam that is not coherent to begin with:3,4

    • G1 is a row of narrow sources. Each opening is coherent across its width, and the wave from each spreads by about λdBL/d by the time it reaches G2.
    • G2 diffracts these waves. Near-field interference behind it builds a density pattern of period d at G3, provided the spacing L is comparable to LT. The pattern from every G1 opening falls in the same place, so the patterns add up.
    • G3 is a mask with the same period. Sliding it sideways and counting the clusters that get through reveals the pattern as a sinusoidal count rate. Its visibility is V = (max − min)/(max + min).

    Clauser proposed this near-field scheme for massive particles in 1997, under the title “De Broglie-wave interference of small rocks and live viruses.”5 It is compact and does not need the nanoradian collimation that far-field diffraction would need at these wavelengths.

    Gratings made of light

    Each grating is a standing wave of 266 nm ultraviolet light, retro-reflected from a mirror, so the period is d = 133 nm. A 266 nm photon has enough energy to ionise a sodium cluster. A cluster that absorbs one is charged and is lost to the neutral beam. At G1 and G3 the light is strong enough that clusters crossing a bright fringe are almost certainly removed: these are photodepletion gratings, and only clusters that cross near a dark fringe get through. G2 is weaker. It removes fewer clusters and mainly imprints a periodic phase through the optical dipole force. Two numbers per grating describe this: n0, the mean number of photons absorbed by a cluster crossing a bright fringe, and φ0, the phase it picks up there:

    n0 = 8σ266PλL / (√(2π) hc wy v),    φ0 = √(8/π) α266P / (ħcε0 wy v),

    with P the laser power, wy the laser waist across the cluster beam, σ266 = (0.537 m/kDa − 1.5) × 10−20 m² the ionisation cross section, and α266 = −4πε0 × 4.5 Å³ per atom the polarisability at 266 nm (negative, so clusters are pushed out of the light).1

    The quantum prediction

    The count rate behind G3 at offset x is a Fourier series, S(x) = Σℓ Sℓ e2πiℓx/d, with

    Sℓ = B(1)−ℓ(0) · B(2)2ℓ(ℓL/LT) · B(3)ℓ(0).

    The Bn(ξ) are the Fourier coefficients of e−n0/2 exp(−ζion cos θ + iζcoh sin θ), with ζcoh = φ0 sin(πξ) and ζion = (n0/2) cos(πξ). They have a closed form in Bessel functions; the app computes them numerically, and the closed form serves as a check. The visibility is V = 2|S1|/S0. All of this is Eqs. 1–7 of the paper.1

    The wave of one cluster

    The top panel shows the wave itself, computed directly rather than through the formula above. Along the beam the motion is fast and steady, so the transverse wave ψ(x, z) obeys the free Schrödinger equation in x with time z/v (the paraxial approximation); each grating multiplies ψ by its transmission t(x) = exp[(−n0/2 + iφ0) cos²(πx/d)].

    The beam reaching G1 comes from a source far upstream through narrow collimating slits. Its transverse coherence width at G1 is about 0.05 nm, far below the period of 133 nm. Each cluster therefore leaves G1 as a point-source wave from one position x0, and the beam is an incoherent mixture of such waves, weighted by the intensity transmission |t1(x0)|². Between G1 and G2 the wave of one cluster is

    ψ(x, z) ∝ z−1/2 exp[ik(x − x0)²/(2z)],   k = 2π/λdB,

    with uniform density: by G2 the wave of a single cluster covers many openings. Write t2(x) = Σn bn e2πinx/d. Each term turns the point-source wave into another point-source wave, from a virtual source at xn = x0 − nλdBL/d. A distance s behind G2, with R = L + s, the wave is therefore exactly

    ψ(x, L + s) ∝ R−1/2 Σn bn e2πinx0/d − iπn²λdBL/d² exp[ik(x − xn)²/(2R)].

    The virtual sources are λdBL/d apart, about 107 nm for 172 kDa clusters: comparable to the period, which is why the waves from neighbouring virtual sources interfere visibly. Summing |ψ|² over x0 and scanning the G3 mask across it reproduces the paper's count rate to better than 0.2% for every case tested; this is an independent check of both calculations.

    The packet

    In the paraxial approximation the full wave factorises, as in the analytical double-slit app: Ψ(x, z, t) = F(z − vt) ψ(x, z) ei(kz − ωt). The transverse pattern ψ(x, z) does not change in time; the longitudinal envelope F carries the cluster along the beam at its speed v. While the simulation runs, the panel lights the pattern with |F|² and dims it behind G2 by the fraction G2 removes. The real envelope is about 0.2 pm long (the paper gives a longitudinal coherence length of 160–350 fm) and crosses the 2 m apparatus in about 12 ms; the packet is drawn about 0.3 m long and slowed so it can be seen. Its length does not affect the transverse pattern in this approximation.

    Each packet carries a few clusters prepared in the same state (Clusters per packet, Advanced). In Pilot Wave they are dots riding inside the packet, at positions drawn from |F|²|ψ|². In Orthodox and Many Worlds, outcomes are drawn from |ψ|² when the packet reaches G2 and G3: ionised (×) or counted (•). The counts in the lower panel gather about 1,600 clusters per scan position and are drawn from the same probabilities.

    When no packet is in flight, the panel shows the density with its brightness normalised in each slice; before G2, where the density is uniform, it is drawn at a fixed low level. Sum over all G1 points (Display) shows the incoherent sum instead of one cluster. The wave is computed for the central mass and speed; the counts and visibilities include the averages.

    Pilot-wave trajectories

    In pilot-wave theory each cluster also has a position, moving with the velocity set by its wave:

    dx/dz = (1/k) Im(∂xψ / ψ),

    evaluated in closed form from the sum above and integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps. Before G2 the trajectories are straight lines from x0. Positions at G2 are uniform, as |ψ|² is there. At G2 and G3 a cluster at x is removed with probability 1 − |t(x)|². This is an effective rule for absorption, as in the analytical double-slit app; the app does not model the ionisation itself. The app checks that the positions reaching G3 are distributed as |ψ|², bin by bin.

    The classical prediction

    Suppose the clusters were classical particles: straight lines, bent only by the dipole force of G2, which acts like an array of tiny lenses. G1 and G3 still form a moiré pattern of shadows. The same formula covers this case with sin(πξ) → πξ and cos(πξ) → 1 (Eqs. 8–9). When L/LT is small, which means heavy or fast clusters, the two predictions merge: the de Broglie wavelength is so short that waves travel like rays. The quantum signature is the difference between the two predictions, and it shows only where L is a sizeable fraction of LT. At 160 m/s that means masses below about 200 kDa.

    What the paper found

    For 172 kDa clusters at the settings of Fig. 2a, the measured visibility was 0.10 ± 0.01 and 0.08 ± 0.01 in two runs. Their dependence on the G2 power follows the quantum prediction, not the classical one. Both predictions are multiplied by a common factor of 0.78 for imperfections: misalignment, vibrations, gravity, rotation and collisions. With the same settings the app gives V = 0.106 for the quantum model and 0.028 for the classical one. From the full data set the authors derive a macroscopicity μ = 15.5,1,6 about ten times beyond the previous record. The measure states how strongly the data rule out a minimal modification of quantum mechanics that would make heavy objects behave classically.

    What the model assumes

    • All numbers are from the arXiv version of the paper (Methods).1 Grating spacing 0.983 m, period 133 nm, speed spread 10 m/s, mass window Δm/m = 0.32.
    • The waist wy. The paper does not state its value. Methods gives the vertical heights of the three grating beams, 620, 575 and 575 µm; the horizontal size is focused to about 20 µm on the mirror. Using 20 µm gives n0 ≈ 75 at G1, which would remove essentially every cluster. Using the vertical heights reproduces the measured visibility, so the app uses those.
    • The simulated scan uses 18 positions spread evenly over two grating periods (steps of 14.8 nm; the paper uses 15 nm), so the sine fit picks out the first harmonic without bias.
    • Speeds are averaged over a Gaussian of ±3σ, and masses over a flat window. The counts behind G3 are simulated by drawing Poisson numbers from the chosen prediction; they are not the measured data.
    • The 0.78 factor is applied equally to both predictions. In the simulated counts it is modelled as a Gaussian smearing of the fringes, which multiplies the ℓ-th harmonic by 0.78ℓ²; the first harmonic, and so the visibility, gets exactly 0.78. The model omits gravity, Earth's rotation, collisions and the thermal emission of the clusters, except through that factor.
    • The fringe profile includes harmonics up to ℓ = 6, so it is not a pure sine when G1 and G3 are strong. The reported visibility is from a sine fit, and the verdict compares it with the two predicted visibilities, as in the paper.

    Why this experiment

    Matter-wave interference started with electrons, atoms and neutrons. In 1999 the Vienna group showed it for C60, a molecule of 720 Da.7 The group later described those experiments for students in the American Journal of Physics.8 The 2026 clusters are about 240 times heavier. Each cluster is metal, about 8 nm across, and in principle visible under a scattering microscope; in the interferometer its centre of mass is spread over a region more than ten times its own size. The app lets you find where the quantum and classical predictions differ, and why higher masses need slower beams.

    What the experiment decides

    The fringes agree with standard quantum mechanics. Any interpretation that reproduces its predictions accounts for them equally well. The experiment does not choose between such interpretations; it constrains theories that predict something different.

    • Collapse theories such as GRW and CSL modify the Schrödinger equation so that superpositions of heavy objects decay. They predict lower visibility for large enough masses. The macroscopicity measure grades experiments by how strongly they rule out such modifications.6 These are distinct theories, and experiments like this one test them.
    • Pilot-wave theory gives each cluster a definite trajectory at all times, guided by the wave.9 The fringes record where those trajectories go after the wave has passed through the gratings. When the paper says the clusters “cannot be assigned a unique trajectory,” that is a statement about the standard formalism, which assigns no trajectories. It is not something the data show.
    • The standard formalism predicts the count rate exactly; it does not say what a cluster does between source and detector.

    Three views of the same wave

    The selector at the top of the controls changes the account, not the physics: the wave, the probabilities and the counts are the same in all three. What differs is what each view says happens to a cluster, and how it reads the gratings. G1 and G3 are measurements in their own right: a cluster that crosses a bright fringe is ionised and removed, and the ones that remain are the ones that were not detected there.

    • Orthodox. The wave gives probabilities for detections. Not being ionised at a grating is itself an outcome, and it updates the wave: ψ is multiplied by t(x). Counting at the detector is a detection. Between gratings the account assigns no position to the cluster, so it gives no picture of what the cluster does there.
    • Pilot Wave. The cluster always has a position, and its wave guides it. Each cluster's trajectory starts at one point of G1 and fans out until G2. Behind G2 the trajectories are drawn into the bright channels of the wave, and they arrive at G3 in fringes. Whether a cluster is ionised at G2 or G3 depends on where it actually is. The panel draws the trajectories of clusters sharing one wave; with Sum over all G1 points on, each trajectory is guided by the wave from its own starting point.
    • Many Worlds. The wave never collapses. Ionisation and counting entangle the cluster with photons, electrons and the detector, and the state branches into records that no longer interfere. The scan you see is one branch; Sample another branch shows another record with the same weights.

    All three give the same counts, and the experiment cannot tell them apart. Spontaneous-collapse theories are different: they predict reduced visibility at high mass, and experiments like this one bound them.
