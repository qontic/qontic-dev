What the app shows

Atoms enter a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. The first beam splitter divides each atom's wave between an upper and a lower arm; mirrors bring the arms together at a second beam splitter. A phase between the arms sets how the recombined wave divides between the detectors. At 0° every atom leaves toward D1 and D2 stays dark; this is the tuning the bomb tester relies on. You can change the phase, remove the second beam splitter, place a which-way marker in the upper arm, or place a bomb there that is destroyed if it absorbs an atom.

The wave

The wave is drawn as Gaussian packets with a carrier wave. Parts of the wave in the same branch are added as complex amplitudes, so where they overlap they form real interference fringes: before the second beam splitter, and where the beams cross when it is removed. Parts in different branches, for example after the which-way marker, are added as densities and show no fringes. The packets do not spread, and mirrors and beam splitters act instantly on the part of a packet that has reached them. The phase φ between the arms sets how the wave divides between D1 and D2: P(D2) = sin²(φ/2).

Statistics

Only the first row depends on the phase. Scanning the phase with nothing in the arms traces the interference fringe, D1 and D2 trading places between 0° and 180°. A marker or a bomb flattens it: the counts no longer depend on the phase. That is what "the interference is gone" means in an interferometer.

The bomb counts do not depend on the phase, but what a click tells you does. A D2 click proves a live bomb only at 0°, where D2 never fires without one; at 180° D1 plays that role. At other phases a click is evidence, not proof: the likelihood ratio is (1/4) / P(click without bomb).

Four accounts

Orthodox. The wave function collapses when a measurement occurs; here, when a detector or the bomb registers the atom. Decoherence explains why the marker removes interference without anyone reading it.

Pilot wave. The atom always has a position and is guided by the wave, which never collapses. With the second beam splitter removed, the trajectories through the crossing are computed from the guiding equation for two freely moving Gaussian packets (ħ = m = 1), including the phase between them. Trajectories never cross each other, so the upper half of the atoms ends at the upper detector D1 for every phase. At 0° and 180° the density is mirror-symmetric and no trajectory crosses the symmetry line; at other phases atoms very close to the axis cross it slightly (by less than a pixel here). With unequal packets the turning point moves. The beam splitter itself is modelled as instantaneous: which output the atom takes depends on where it sits in its packet, with trajectories keeping their order, so the top fraction cos²(φ/2) of the atoms goes to D1.

Many worlds. The wave function never collapses. A record, while it persists, splits it into branches that no longer interfere. The app shows each split as copies of the whole apparatus, labelled with their Born weights, and then follows the copy that matches the detector you see. With nothing in the arms at 0° or 180°, only one outcome has weight and the world does not split; at other phases it splits at the detectors.

Spontaneous collapse. A modified theory (GRW and related models): collapse is a physical process, extremely rare for one atom and effectively immediate once many particles are displaced, as in a detector or a bomb. The collapse clock uses the original GRW rate, 10−16 s−1 per nucleon: one rubidium-87 atom alone would wait about 3.6 million years (3.2 million with a microscopic marker of about a dozen extra nucleons), while a detector or bomb with about 1023 nucleons displaced collapses within about 100 ns. A microscopic marker adds only a few nucleons, so collapse still waits for the detector. It predicts small deviations from standard quantum mechanics; experiments have narrowed its parameters and excluded some versions.

Experiments and limits

The bomb tester was proposed by Elitzur and Vaidman (1993) and realized with photons by Kwiat et al. (1995). Wheeler's delayed-choice experiment has been done with photons (Jacques et al. 2007) and single atoms (Manning et al. 2015). Student difficulties with the beam-splitter and which-way questions are documented for photon interferometers (Marshman & Singh 2016). The app uses atoms because pilot wave has no accepted theory of photon trajectories.
