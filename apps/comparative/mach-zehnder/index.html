What the app shows

Atoms enter a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. The first beam splitter divides each atom's wave between an upper and a lower arm; mirrors bring the arms together at a second beam splitter, which is tuned so that every atom leaves toward D1 and D2 stays dark. You can remove the second beam splitter, place a which-way marker in the upper arm, or place a bomb there that is destroyed if it absorbs an atom.

The wave

The wave is drawn as Gaussian packets with a carrier wave. Parts of the wave in the same branch are added as complex amplitudes, so where they overlap they form real interference fringes: before the second beam splitter, and where the beams cross when it is removed. Parts in different branches, for example after the which-way marker, are added as densities and show no fringes. The packets do not spread, and mirrors and beam splitters act instantly on the part of a packet that has reached them. The phase between the arms (Advanced tab) sets how the wave divides between D1 and D2: P(D2) = sin²(φ/2).

Four accounts

Orthodox. The wave function collapses when a measurement occurs; here, when a detector or the bomb registers the atom. Decoherence explains why the marker removes interference without anyone reading it.

Pilot wave. The atom always has a position and is guided by the wave, which never collapses. With the second beam splitter removed, the trajectories through the crossing are computed from the guiding equation for two freely moving Gaussian packets (ħ = m = 1); they cannot cross the symmetry line. The beam splitter itself is modelled as instantaneous: which output the atom takes depends on where it sits in its packet.

Many worlds. The wave function never collapses. A record, while it persists, splits it into branches that no longer interfere.

Spontaneous collapse. A modified theory (GRW and related models): collapse is a physical process, extremely rare for one atom and effectively immediate once many particles are displaced, as in a detector or a bomb. It predicts small deviations from standard quantum mechanics; experiments have narrowed its parameters and excluded some versions.

Experiments and limits

The bomb tester was proposed by Elitzur and Vaidman (1993) and realized with photons by Kwiat et al. (1995). Wheeler's delayed-choice experiment has been done with photons (Jacques et al. 2007) and single atoms (Manning et al. 2015). Student difficulties with the beam-splitter and which-way questions are documented for photon interferometers (Marshman & Singh 2016). The app uses atoms because pilot wave has no accepted theory of photon trajectories.
