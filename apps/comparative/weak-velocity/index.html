The setup

  1. Particles go through the double slit one at a time, as in the double-slit app.
  2. At the weak plane each particle is coupled to a separate pointer. The pointer moves by the particle's transverse position, but its own starting position is spread out, so one reading says almost nothing.
  3. A short gap later, at the strong plane, a detector records where the particle is.
  4. Each run gives two numbers: the pointer reading and the detected position. Nothing else is recorded.

The idea. Keep only the runs in which the particle was detected in one narrow strip: this is post-selection. Average their pointer readings. Detected position minus that mean pointer reading, divided by the time between the planes, is taken as the velocity there. For a very wide pointer the mean reading tends to the real part of the weak value of the position at the weak plane. Whether it tells where those particles were is one of the points on which the views differ (Rationale tab).

The setup (Windows method)

  1. Particles go through the double slit one at a time.
  2. At an instant t₁, chosen at random for each run, each particle is coupled to a separate pointer that reads its position, along and across the beam. The pointer's own starting position is spread out, so one reading says almost nothing.
  3. A short time τ later, at t₂ = t₁ + τ, a detector records where the particle is.
  4. Each run gives two positions: the pointer reading at t₁ and the detected position at t₂.

The idea. Collect many runs, then choose a small window of detected positions. Keep only the runs detected in it (post-selection) and average their pointer readings. The arrow from that mean reading to the mean detected position, over τ, is taken as the velocity there.

In brief

  • Standard quantum mechanics (any upper-level course): the double-slit wave, the probability density |ψ|², the probability current j and the Born rule for detection.
  • Beyond the textbook: a weak measurement couples a pointer so loosely that one reading says little; averaging the pointer over runs that end in the same detected strip gives a velocity. With a weak pointer and many runs it equals j/|ψ|².
  • Where views differ: all three predict the same arrows and lines; they differ on whether the lines describe paths (Rationale tab).
  • Two methods, the same three steps: measuring at fixed planes, as in the photon experiment (Method: Planes), or at random times with the full position, as Wiseman wrote it (Method: Windows), gives the same field. Each method has one run, many runs, and a map.
  • Windows: runs at all times fill the whole field; a small window of strong results, the mean weak reading of the runs that ended there, and the arrow between them give the velocity anywhere. Repeating the step traces a line back to the slits.

The wave

The wave is that of the analytical double-slit app (and, with more slits, the analytical N-slit app: Advanced → Number of slits, 1–8, equally spaced, same Gaussian apertures), with the same default settings (wavelength 50 nm, Gaussian slits of width σ = 30 nm, 300 nm apart, screen 1000 nm behind the slits). The Advanced tab changes the geometry and the beam within the same ranges and limits as that app. Behind the slits the transverse wave is a sum of two complex Gaussians and evolves exactly in the paraxial approximation: the distance x from the slits plays the role of time, τ = x/vx, and the forward velocity vx = ħk/m is the same everywhere. The simulation uses the same coefficients as that app; a test checks that the two waves agree.

The weak measurement

At the weak plane the particle is coupled to a pointer, a separate system with coordinate Y. Before the coupling the pointer's wave is a Gaussian of spread σp centred on zero. The coupling shifts it by the particle's transverse position y:

ψ(y) φ(Y)  →  ψ(y) φ(Y − y).

Y is the pointer's coordinate, not the particle's. The pointer starts with a preparation spread σp; Y is the coordinate recorded at readout. With the default σp = 200 nm this is comparable to the whole interference pattern, so one reading tells almost nothing. In exchange the particle's wave is barely disturbed. A small σp gives a precise reading and a strong disturbance: try 10 nm.

The strong measurement and post-selection

After a gap Δx the particle's transverse position is measured precisely, giving yf. The joint probability of the two readings is computed exactly:

P(Y, yf) = |χY(yf, t₁ + τ)|²,   χY(y, t₁) = ψ(y, t₁) φ(Y − y),

where t₁ is the time of the weak coupling and χY evolves freely for τ = gap/vx, the time between the two measurements. Each run samples one pair (Y, yf) from this distribution. Post-selection keeps the runs whose yf falls in a narrow strip (20 nm by default; strips remain at most 20 nm wide when the screen changes) and averages their pointer readings. Following Wiseman,1 the velocity at yf is defined as

vy(yf) = [ yf − E(Y | yf) ] / τ.

The simulation shows it as the direction vy/vx, the slope of the arrow. Each arrow is the measured vector itself: it runs from the mean pointer reading, at the weak position, to the mean detected position of the same runs, at the strong position (where the gap is too short on screen, the tail is extended back along the same direction). Its tip marks the strip's mean detected position; the drawing is stretched across the beam unless Equal scales is on, so its drawn angle is steeper than its slope. Its standard error is the sample standard deviation of the paired differences yf − Y in the strip, divided by √N and by Δτ. This includes the correlation between the two readings. With a wide pointer it is approximately σp/(√N Δτ). The default collects one million runs per pair of planes; each arrow uses only the runs detected in its strip, so the precision varies across the pattern.

What it converges to

As σp grows, E(Y | yf) tends to the real part of the weak value of the earlier position,2 and vy tends to

vy = Im(∂yψ / ψ) ħ/m = j / |ψ|²,

the probability current divided by the density at the strong plane. In Griffiths' notation the transverse current is j = (ħ/m) Im(ψ* ∂ψ/∂y), so j/|ψ|² = (ħ/m) Im(∂yψ/ψ); E(Y | yf) is the mean pointer reading over the runs detected at yf, a conditional expectation value. The weak value itself is complex; the pointer's mean position returns only its real part, and the imaginary part, which is not zero here, plays no role. The particle moves freely between the two planes, so y(τ) = y(τ + Δτ) − p Δτ/m holds as an operator identity, and in the limit of a very wide pointer the result is exact for any gap, not only for Δx → 0.

With a finite pointer the gap matters. A longer gap gives a larger signal, but also a larger bias, because the pointer's disturbance has longer to act. A shorter gap reduces the bias but not the noise: the displacement shrinks with the gap while the pointer's spread does not, so the error of each arrow grows as 1/Δx, and a gap of 20 nm needs about six times the runs of a 50 nm gap for the same precision. The lines through the arrows inherit this noise, most where the velocity changes steeply and few runs land, as near the dark fringes. At σp = 50 nm and a 100 nm gap the bias reaches about a tenth of the largest velocity; at 200 nm it stays near 1%. The blue curve in the velocity plot is the prediction for the chosen pointer and gap, with infinitely many runs; the dashed curve is the weak limit j/|ψ|². Below about 50 nm they separate visibly.

The Results panel compares strip averages: both predictions are averaged over the same strip, weighted by the density. Next to a node of the pattern the strip average can differ from the value of j/|ψ|² at the strip centre by several percent, because the velocity changes quickly there. In a dark fringe |ψ|² is small (in the default geometry about a fifth of the peak at the strong plane; it vanishes only where the two slits' waves have equal size and opposite phase) and j/|ψ|² changes steeply there; few runs land there, the error bars are large, and the plots show a sharp peak. The finite σp is not the only reason the curves differ near nodes.

Why the field does not depend on time

In this model the wave factorizes as Ψ = F(x − vxt) φ(y; x) ei(kx − ωt): a Gaussian longitudinal envelope F (σ = 50 nm, as in the double-slit app) that only translates, times a transverse wave φ that depends on the plane x. F cancels in Im(∂yψ/ψ), so the velocity at a point is the same whenever a packet passes it, and runs need not be timed from their release. The same holds for a steady, nearly monochromatic beam, such as the single-photon source of Kocsis et al. It fails for packets short enough that their spread of wavelengths matters: they stretch as they travel, the velocity at a point then depends on time, and post-selection would also have to condition on the arrival time. The simulation therefore draws a continuous beam; runs are events at random times. The Show control draws the density, travelling crests, or the phase as colour.

Planes, and measuring at times

Each line in the drawing stands for a detector that covers the whole transverse range at a fixed distance from the slits. It records the transverse position of every particle that reaches it; the longitudinal position is not recorded. The points are drawn just beside the lines only so they can be seen. This is also how Kocsis et al. did it: a camera imaged one plane at a time, each photon was registered by the pixel it hit, and nothing was timed.3

Wiseman wrote the protocol with the two measurements at two times, a weak one at t and a strong one a short time τ later, in the limit τ → 0.1 At a fixed time the particle can be anywhere along its packet, so a measurement at an instant covers a range of planes, not one. Recorded with the full position, including the position along the beam, and post-selected on it, such measurements give the same field as the planes. The Windows method does exactly this: runs at all times, the full position recorded, post-selection on any region of strong results. If only the transverse position were used, each arrow would average the field over the packet's length; for a 150 nm packet, at the default planes, the slopes would change by up to about a quarter of the largest slope. Earlier versions offered that as an option; it was removed, because it is neither the Kocsis experiment, which used planes, nor Wiseman's protocol, which uses the full position.

What the measurement does to the wave

The coupling entangles the particle with the pointer: after it, the state is ψ(y) φ(Y − y), not a product. For a given reading Y, the particle's wave is ψ(y) φ(Y − y), which is ψ cut down to a window of width σp around Y; this conditional wave is what then reaches the strong plane, and it is what the joint probability P(Y, yf) is built from. In stage 1 the drawing shows, beyond the weak plane, the wave of the current run. With a 200 nm pointer the window is wider than the pattern and the wave looks almost unchanged; with 10 nm it is a narrow beam around the reading. Averaged over all readings, the density at the weak plane is unchanged, but downstream the interference between parts of the wave farther apart than about σp is lost: the density matrix is multiplied by exp[−(y − y′)²/(8σp²)]. Stage 2 draws that averaged density beyond the weak plane. It is a mixture, with no single wave, so no crests or phase are drawn there. A test checks it against the table of P(Y, yf).

The Windows method

The Windows method (Standard level and up) does the measurement the other way round: instead of choosing a pair of planes first, it collects runs at all times and chooses afterwards. Each run is one packet, measured weakly at an instant t₁ and strongly at t₂ = t₁ + τ, as in Wiseman's protocol. Choosing t₁ at random from run to run is this app's way of covering the whole field; it works because here the field does not change with time (see above). The weak pointer reads both coordinates; the strong measurement records the full position. Because the packet only translates, a run whose strong measurement finds the particle at x₂ along the beam is a run at the pair of planes x₂ − gap and x₂, so in the simulation the field is a stack of thin plane pairs 10 nm apart, all equally likely, each with its own exact joint distribution. Its three steps mirror the planes: One run shows single runs, each at its own random instants; Many runs collects 20 million runs and lets you point a window anywhere; Map the field draws, from the same runs, arrows from windows on a grid and lines traced back from the screen. Along the beam the weak reading averages to x₂ − vxτ exactly (the packet does not stretch), so that reading only adds noise of the same spread σp. With the default 200 nm pointer a single weak reading can therefore land far from the t₁ line, even beyond the later detection; only averages carry the velocity.

A window is a region of strong results: an ellipse, by default 100 nm long along the beam and 10 nm wide across it. The runs that ended in it give two averages: the mean weak position (X̄, Ȳ) and the mean strong position. Their difference over τ is the measured velocity; the slope shown is the difference across divided by the difference along. The individual weak readings scatter over hundreds of nanometres (the amber cloud); only their mean is sharp. The window is not only drawn: it decides which runs are averaged, as the strip does in step 2 of the Planes method, so its shape changes the result. The prediction for the same window and pointer is computed from the exact distributions. It differs from j/|ψ|² at the window's centre where the velocity changes within the window. Across the beam the velocity changes fast near the fringes; along the beam it changes slowly. A window narrow across and long along therefore follows the field closely and still collects runs. At the window centred at (550 nm, 50 nm), where j/|ψ|² gives −0.372, the prediction is −0.360 for the default 100 × 10 nm ellipse and −0.246 for a circle 50 nm across, which collects about three times as many runs. With 20 million runs the default window holds about 10,000 runs there and the error in the slope is about 0.04. Cells at the window's edge count by the fraction of them inside, so the window's size does not jump as it moves.

Clicking a window traces back from it: the mean weak position becomes the next window, one gap at a time, until the slits. This is the construction Anssi Kuha used in the simulations that led to this app. It is compared with the flow line of j/|ψ|² followed backward from the same window. Followed forward from the slits instead, small differences fan out with the pattern, so that comparison would mostly show the fan-out. The two need not end at the same point. Most of the difference is systematic: each window averages the velocity over its area, which matters where it changes steeply. With exact averages (infinitely many runs), traces from seven starting windows end 2–20 nm from the flow line with a circle 50 nm across, within about 8 nm with a circle 20 nm across, and within about 4.5 nm with the default 100 × 10 nm ellipse. Sampling noise adds a few nanometres more, accumulated over the steps; the pointer's disturbance matters little at 200 nm. With 20 million runs and the default window, the twenty lines drawn at the end of the run end 3.2 nm (rms) from the flow lines; with the 50 nm circle, 16 nm. As in the planes map, no particle is followed: every step uses different runs.

Times are shown in picoseconds for an electron with the chosen wavelength (v = h/mλ, about 14.5 km/s at 50 nm). Only these numbers depend on the mass; the rest of the app uses lengths. With the Planes method, at the defaults, the weak coupling happens 34.4 ps after the packet centre leaves the slits and the strong measurement 3.44 ps later. With the Windows method t₁ changes from run to run; only τ = t₂ − t₁ (3.44 ps at the defaults) is fixed.

Step 3, Map the field: the velocity field and its flow lines

With the Planes method, step 3 repeats the experiment at twelve pairs of planes from the slits to the screen, each with fresh runs. The arrows give a measured velocity field. Starting points divide the detected distribution at the first strong plane into equal-probability intervals. They are taken from a fine histogram, including when the screen accepts only part of the wave. Each line follows the measured arrows, interpolated between planes and strips. No particle is followed through all twelve pairs: joining the arrows is a step in the analysis. Amber ticks show where the reconstructed lines reach the screen; grey ticks show the ends of the exact j/|ψ|² flow lines from the same starts. The caption reports their root-mean-square difference and how many lines reach the screen. An open circle marks a line that stops where measured velocities are unavailable. This compares the reconstruction with the prepared-state flow; agreement is predicted by all three views.

The optional dashed lines are the flow lines of j/|ψ|² computed directly from ψ, from the same starts. They are the same curves as the pilot-wave trajectories of the double-slit app. Agreement between the two sets checks the measurement and its statistics.

A related measurement was done by Kocsis et al. with single photons.3 It differs from this one in two ways. They weakly measured the photon's transverse momentum, through its polarization in a birefringent crystal, rather than its position over a gap (the scheme here is Wiseman's1). And photons are not massive Schrödinger particles; the paraxial wave equation for light has the same form, which is why the comparison works. They called the lines “average trajectories” of the photons, noted that trajectories measured this way reproduce those of Bohm's theory, and stressed that the reconstruction does not depend on a choice of interpretation. This app simulates the scheme Wiseman proposed for a massive particle,1 with an idealized apparatus; it does not reproduce an experiment.

Kocsis et al. detected about 31,000 photons per exposure and reconstructed the field from a series of imaging planes. They measured momentum through a weak polarization coupling, rather than inferring velocity from a small position difference over a gap. The statistical requirements of the two apparatuses therefore cannot be compared by counts alone. Their coupling was weak enough to leave the spatial interference nearly unchanged. Because the coupling commutes with free propagation, the crystal could remain fixed while different imaging planes were examined; this does not mean that a strong momentum measurement would preserve the later position statistics. For light, Bliokh et al. read the same kind of weak momentum measurement as a measurement of the Poynting vector of classical optics.8

What the model assumes

  • Units with ħ = m = 1 inside the code; positions are shown in nm.
  • The coupling is impulsive and the pointer does not evolve between coupling and reading. With the Planes method only the particle's transverse coordinate is coupled. The Windows method also reads the coordinate along the beam; since the packet only translates, that reading is modelled as the particle's position along the beam plus pointer noise of the same spread, with no back-action on the motion along the beam.
  • Why only the transverse position (Planes). In the paraxial model the forward velocity is the same everywhere, vx = ħk/m, and the packet only translates. Only vy needs measuring; vx is known. Measuring at a plane gives the position along the beam for free (the plane's). Kocsis et al. likewise measured only the transverse momentum. Beyond this model (wide angles, or packets short enough to stretch as they travel) vx varies, and both coordinates would have to be measured weakly. The Windows method does read both, as Wiseman's protocol does; here the reading along the beam only adds noise, because its mean is fixed by the speed.
  • The pointer reading Y is a pointer coordinate, drawn on the same scale as y. A 200 nm pointer reads outside the drawn range in about one run in eight; those readings are counted at the edge of the drawing, and all of them enter the averages.
  • A sharp pointer adds transverse momentum of spread ħ/(2σp). Relative to forward momentum this is λ/(4πσp). At the default λ = 50 nm it is about 0.4 for a 10 nm pointer, so the sharp-pointer demonstration is qualitative beyond the controlled paraxial range. The Gaussian solution remains exact within the reduced model.
  • The joint distribution is tabulated across the screen (±400 nm), with finer cells for sharper pointers and wider screens. At each detected position, the pointer grid covers the conditional Gaussian envelopes to eight standard deviations. This includes readings outside the detector window. Tests check domain and grid convergence as well as sampled strip averages.
  • Particles that hit the wall are not counted; the experiment starts behind the slits.
  • The wave is drawn all the way to the screen although the strong measurement detects the particle earlier. The drawing shows the prepared wave for the whole arrangement; in each run the particle is detected at the strong plane (or instant), and nothing of that run continues beyond it.

For instructors

Activity, questions and instructor notes

The main point. Each run gives a pointer reading Y and a detected position yf. Averaging Y over the runs detected in one strip, and comparing with yf, gives a velocity. With a weak pointer and enough runs it is j/|ψ|², and the lines through the arrows are its flow lines. A sharp pointer gives different arrows, because it disturbs the wave.

The common mistake. Using one noisy pointer reading to establish a particle's path. The average of all pointer readings gives the prepared mean position. To reconstruct velocity, average readings over runs with the same final detected position. The signal is a few nanometres against a 200 nm initial pointer spread; the yf − Y button on the data plot (Expert level) magnifies it.

A sequence (about 25 minutes). Everything up to the maps works at the Basic level, which a first-time visitor sees; links that set more (such as runs) open at Standard. Stage 1, five minutes: a few runs at 200 nm, then at 10 nm. Stage 2, ten minutes: before running, hide the two predictions in Display (this also hides their Results values) and ask students to predict the sign of vy in the strip 40–60 nm, just off the centre line; then run and compare. Stage 3, eight minutes: map with 200 nm, then repeat with 10 nm (1 million runs per plane pair at Basic; 10 million, at Standard, gives cleaner lines and takes about 40 s per map). Optional, at Standard: Method → Windows, the same three steps at random instants, to point anywhere and trace back.

Questions, with expected answers.

  1. Why does one reading with a 200 nm pointer tell little, and why is that useful? Its spread is comparable to the whole pattern; in exchange the coupling barely disturbs the wave, so the averages describe the undisturbed experiment.
  2. With the default settings (weak plane 500 nm, gap 50 nm), what is the sign of vy in the strip 40–60 nm, just above the centre line? Negative, toward the centre: the prediction is −0.34. At 1 million runs about 1.3% of runs land there and the error is about ±0.035, so a class will see values between roughly −0.27 and −0.41. Most students expect "outward". (At 300–320 nm the slope is +0.25 ± 0.05, outward.) Link: ?stage=many&runs=1000000
  3. Find the pointer spread at which the measured arrows depart visibly from j/|ψ|². Below about 50 nm the blue and dashed curves separate; at 10 nm they differ by more than the error bars over most of the screen. Link: ?stage=many&sw=10
  4. How many runs in one strip are needed for an error of 0.01 in the slope with a 200 nm pointer and a 50 nm gap? The error is about σp/(√N Δx) = 4/√N, so N ≈ 160 000 runs in the strip, about 5 million in total for either strip next to the centre.
  5. With the Windows method (step 2), set the window to a circle 50 nm across (length and width 50 nm) at (550 nm, 50 nm). Why does its slope, about −0.25, differ from j/|ψ|² at the centre, −0.37? Then make it 100 nm long and 10 nm wide. The circle averages the field over 50 nm across, where the velocity changes steeply; the long, narrow window averages along the beam, where it hardly changes, and gives −0.36 while still collecting about a third as many runs. Link: ?stage=window&winl=50&winw=50
  6. Do lines reconstructed from many separate runs establish the path of one measured particle? No. All three views predict the same reconstructed field. In pilot-wave theory its ideal weak-limit flow lines are guidance trajectories; that identification is not required to reconstruct the field. Link: ?stage=map
Trying this with students

Start with a few students working individually for about 20 minutes. Ask them to explain what they are thinking while you observe. Give the questions before showing the instructor answers above.

  1. After one run: what are the two recorded numbers? Can you infer a path from them?
  2. Before many runs: predict what will become more stable as the count grows. Explain what selecting a strip does.
  3. After the field map: was any particle measured at all twelve pairs? Does agreement with pilot-wave trajectories distinguish the three views?

Note where students get stuck and which words or controls they misread. Repeat the same questions at the end in their own words. Use this first tryout to improve the explanation; it is not yet a test of learning effectiveness.

Levels. The Level switch above Start offers Basic, Standard and Expert. Basic shows the Planes method with the pointer spread only; Standard adds the Method switch (Windows), the planes, the number of runs, the window's size, the geometry and the display modes; Expert adds the map settings and the yf − Y view. A setting the level hides takes its default value; yours comes back when you return to a level that shows it. First-time visitors start at Basic; links that use Standard or Expert settings open at that level.

Quiz. Eight predict-before-you-watch questions, written from the misreadings students reported: one run read as a velocity, the window read as a region of weak readings, a sharp pointer expected to do better, a bigger window expected to do better, the trace back read as a path, agreement with j/|ψ|² read as support for one interpretation, and the two methods expected to differ. Each opens the matching state in the simulation. A useful class opening: question 1 before any run, question 6 after the Windows step 2.

What is measured

The data are pairs of numbers: a pointer reading and a detected position. The velocity is an average over a post-selected set of runs, so it is not a property read off any single particle. The same data, and the same lines, follow from the Schrödinger equation alone. The views differ on what the lines are. Kocsis et al., who measured such lines for photons, call them “average trajectories”.3

Three readings of the same lines

Orthodox

All the data are in the joint distribution P(Y, yf): the scatter plot. An arrow is a regression of Y on yf, and the weak value is a statistic of that distribution. Quantum mechanics predicts it and it can be measured, but it is not a property of any single particle; in other set-ups weak values can even lie outside the range of possible results.2 Each run gives a weak reading and a strong result at one pair of planes. No particle is followed through the whole sequence of plane pairs: joining the arrows is the analyst's step, and the line summarizes an ensemble. j/|ψ|² is a property of the prepared state, not of any particle.

Pilot-wave

Each particle has a position and moves with velocity j/|ψ|². For a weak pointer the procedure returns that velocity at the strong plane, and the lines coincide with the guidance trajectories of the double-slit app.1,5 The pointer coupling also acts on the particle, so with a finite pointer and gap the agreement is only approximate, and with a sharp pointer it fails (try 10 nm). Within the theory the agreement is a consistency result, not a test of it. Turn on the flow-line overlay to compare.

Many-worlds

There is no additional particle position beyond the wave, and these lines are not individual particle trajectories. |ψ|² at a plane gives the weights the branches will have once a detector there records a position; the arrows summarize how that distribution changes from plane to plane, and their flow lines are a derived description. Each post-selected average is a conditional expectation relative to the branch in which the detector reports yf.

The trace back of the Windows method. The chain of windows is built in the analysis in all three readings: each dot is the mean weak position of a different set of runs, and no particle is followed. Orthodox: a chain of conditional averages, each a statistic of P(Y, yf) for one window. Pilot-wave: for a weak pointer and a small window, the chain approximates the trajectory that passes through the window. Many-worlds: a line of the flow of |ψ|², which gives the branch weights. Its agreement with the j/|ψ|² flow line is predicted by all three and does not decide between them.

A limit of the procedure

In the ideal weak limit, the protocol returns j/|ψ|² for all empirically equivalent guidance laws. Pilot-wave variants that add to the velocity a term which does not change |ψ|² predict the same data, so the experiment cannot single out the standard guidance law. Wiseman himself argues that this operational, “naively observable” velocity is the most natural way to define the velocity field, and that if particles move deterministically it gives the standard guidance law; that is the sense of his paper's title.1 Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì argue that within standard pilot-wave theory it is still a genuine measurement of the particle's velocity, and that it would not measure the velocity of such a variant.4

What the lines do not settle

Agreement between the measured lines and the flow lines of j/|ψ|² is predicted by all three views, so it does not decide between them. Nor does it say that the lines are where particles go in every arrangement: with which-way markers, Bohmian trajectories can disagree with what the marker records, the “surrealistic trajectories” of Englert et al.6 Mahler et al. reconstructed such trajectories with weak measurements and traced the disagreement to the nonlocality of the guidance law.7 Other readings of the same data do not involve trajectories at all. For light, Bliokh et al. read weak momentum measurements as measurements of the energy flow (Poynting vector) of classical optics.8 Vaidman uses weak traces, within standard quantum mechanics with pre- and post-selection, to say where a particle has been, without joining them into paths.9

Why it belongs here

The double-slit app draws the flow lines of j/|ψ|² from the theory. This app shows that the same field can be reconstructed from data that could be taken in a laboratory. Whether the lines are paths is what the three views dispute. It also shows the price: a weak pointer, post-selection and many runs per pair of planes, with only a fraction contributing to each arrow. The idea and the first version of these pictures are Anssi Kuha's.
