The setup

  1. Particles go through the double slit one at a time, as in the double-slit app.
  2. At the weak plane each particle is coupled to a separate pointer. The pointer moves by the particle's transverse position, but its own starting position is spread out, so one reading says almost nothing.
  3. A short gap later, at the strong plane, a detector records where the particle is.
  4. Each run gives two numbers: the pointer reading and the detected position. Nothing else is recorded.

The idea. Keep only the runs in which the particle was detected in one narrow strip: this is post-selection. Average their pointer readings. Detected position minus that mean pointer reading, divided by the time between the planes, is taken as the velocity there. For a very wide pointer the mean reading tends to the real part of the weak value of the position at the weak plane. Whether it tells where those particles were is one of the points on which the views differ (Rationale tab).

The wave

The wave is that of the analytical double-slit app, with the same default settings (wavelength 50 nm, Gaussian slits of width σ = 30 nm, 300 nm apart, screen 1000 nm behind the slits). The Advanced tab changes the geometry and the beam within the same ranges and limits as that app. Behind the slits the transverse wave is a sum of two complex Gaussians and evolves exactly in the paraxial approximation: the distance x from the slits plays the role of time, τ = x/vx, and the forward velocity vx = ħk/m is the same everywhere. The simulation uses the same coefficients as that app; a test checks that the two waves agree.

The weak measurement

At the weak plane the particle is coupled to a pointer, a separate system with coordinate Y. Before the coupling the pointer's wave is a Gaussian of spread σp centred on zero. The coupling shifts it by the particle's transverse position y:

ψ(y) φ(Y)  →  ψ(y) φ(Y − y).

Y is the pointer's coordinate, not the particle's. A single reading of Y has an uncertainty σp. With the default σp = 200 nm this is comparable to the whole interference pattern, so one reading tells almost nothing. In exchange the particle's wave is barely disturbed. A small σp gives a precise reading and a strong disturbance: try 10 nm.

The strong measurement and post-selection

After a gap Δx the particle's transverse position is measured precisely, giving yf. The joint probability of the two readings is computed exactly:

P(Y, yf) = |χY(yf, τ + Δτ)|²,   χY(y, τ) = ψ(y, τ) φ(Y − y),

where χY evolves freely for Δτ = Δx/vx. Each run samples one pair (Y, yf) from this distribution. Post-selection keeps the runs whose yf falls in a narrow strip (20 nm, one fortieth of the screen) and averages their pointer readings. Following Wiseman,1 the velocity at yf is defined as

vy(yf) = [ yf − E(Y | yf) ] / Δτ.

The simulation shows it as the direction vy/vx, the slope of the arrow. Its standard error is the sample standard deviation of the paired differences yf − Y in the strip, divided by √N and by Δτ. This includes the correlation between the two readings. With a wide pointer it is approximately σp/(√N Δτ). The default collects one million runs per pair of planes; each arrow uses only the runs detected in its strip, so the precision varies across the pattern.

What it converges to

As σp grows, E(Y | yf) tends to the real part of the weak value of the earlier position,2 and vy tends to

vy = Im(∂yψ / ψ) ħ/m = j / |ψ|²,

the probability current divided by the density at the strong plane. The weak value itself is complex; the pointer's mean position returns only its real part, and the imaginary part, which is not zero here, plays no role. The particle moves freely between the two planes, so y(τ) = y(τ + Δτ) − p Δτ/m holds as an operator identity, and in the limit of a very wide pointer the result is exact for any gap, not only for Δx → 0.

With a finite pointer the gap matters. A longer gap gives a larger signal, but also a larger bias, because the pointer's disturbance has longer to act. A shorter gap reduces the bias but not the noise: the displacement shrinks with the gap while the pointer's spread does not, so the error of each arrow grows as 1/Δx, and a gap of 20 nm needs about six times the runs of a 50 nm gap for the same precision. The lines through the arrows inherit this noise, most where the velocity changes steeply and few runs land, as near the dark fringes. At σp = 50 nm and a 100 nm gap the bias reaches about a tenth of the largest velocity; at 200 nm it stays near 1%. The green curve in the velocity plot is the prediction for the chosen pointer and gap, with infinitely many runs; the dashed curve is the weak limit j/|ψ|². Below about 50 nm they separate visibly.

The Results panel compares strip averages: both predictions are averaged over the same strip, weighted by the density. Next to a node of the pattern the strip average can differ from the value of j/|ψ|² at the strip centre by several percent, because the velocity changes quickly there. In a dark fringe |ψ|² is small (in the default geometry about a fifth of the peak at the strong plane; it vanishes only where the two slits' waves have equal size and opposite phase) and j/|ψ|² changes steeply there; few runs land there, the error bars are large, and the plots show a sharp peak. The finite σp is not the only reason the curves differ near nodes.

Why the field does not depend on time

In this model the wave factorizes as Ψ = F(x − vxt) φ(y; x) ei(kx − ωt): a Gaussian longitudinal envelope F (σ = 50 nm, as in the double-slit app) that only translates, times a transverse wave φ that depends on the plane x. F cancels in Im(∂yψ/ψ), so the velocity at a point is the same whenever a packet passes it, and runs need not be timed from their release. The same holds for a steady, nearly monochromatic beam, such as the single-photon source of Kocsis et al. It fails for packets short enough that their spread of wavelengths matters: they stretch as they travel, the velocity at a point then depends on time, and post-selection would also have to condition on the arrival time. The simulation therefore draws a continuous beam; runs are events at random times. The Show control draws the density, travelling crests, or the phase as colour.

What the measurement does to the wave

The coupling entangles the particle with the pointer: after it, the state is ψ(y) φ(Y − y), not a product. For a given reading Y, the particle's wave is ψ(y) φ(Y − y), which is ψ cut down to a window of width σp around Y; this conditional wave is what then reaches the strong plane, and it is what the joint probability P(Y, yf) is built from. In stage 1 the drawing shows, beyond the weak plane, the wave of the current run. With a 200 nm pointer the window is wider than the pattern and the wave looks almost unchanged; with 10 nm it is a narrow beam around the reading. Averaged over all readings, the density at the weak plane is unchanged, but downstream the interference between parts of the wave farther apart than about σp is lost: the density matrix is multiplied by exp[−(y − y′)²/(8σp²)]. Stage 2 draws that averaged density beyond the weak plane. It is a mixture, with no single wave, so no crests or phase are drawn there. A test checks it against the table of P(Y, yf).

Stage 3: the velocity field and its flow lines

Stage 3 repeats the experiment at twelve pairs of planes from the slits to the screen, each with fresh runs. The arrows give a measured velocity field. Starting points are placed at equal-probability positions in the first strong plane, and each line follows the measured arrows, interpolated linearly between planes and between strips. No run is measured at more than one pair of planes; joining the arrows is a step taken in the analysis. If the arrows are right, the lines end at equal-probability positions on the screen. That holds for the flow lines of any velocity field that carries |ψ|² (the continuity equation), so it checks the arrows, not an interpretation. The ticks on the right show where the measured lines end (amber) and the equal-probability positions (grey); the caption gives their root-mean-square difference for the lines that reach the screen.

The optional dashed lines are the flow lines of j/|ψ|² computed directly from ψ, from the same starts. They are the same curves as the pilot-wave trajectories of the double-slit app. Agreement between the two sets checks the measurement and its statistics.

A related measurement was done by Kocsis et al. with single photons.3 It differs from this one in two ways. They weakly measured the photon's transverse momentum, through its polarization in a birefringent crystal, rather than its position over a gap (the scheme here is Wiseman's1). And photons are not massive Schrödinger particles; the paraxial wave equation for light has the same form, which is why the comparison works. They called the lines “average trajectories” of the photons, noted that trajectories measured this way reproduce those of Bohm's theory, and stressed that the reconstruction does not depend on a choice of interpretation. This app simulates the scheme Wiseman proposed for a massive particle,1 with an idealized apparatus; it does not reproduce an experiment.

Kocsis et al. detected about 31,000 photons per exposure and reconstructed the field at 41 planes. They measured momentum through a weak polarization coupling, rather than inferring velocity from a small position difference over a gap. The statistical requirements of the two apparatuses therefore cannot be compared by counts alone. Their coupling was weak enough to leave the spatial interference nearly unchanged. Because the coupling commutes with free propagation, the crystal could remain fixed while different imaging planes were examined; this does not mean that a strong momentum measurement would preserve the later position statistics. Their trajectories are still noisy; they attribute the bunching of trajectories in the bright fringes to measurement noise. For light, Bliokh et al. read the same kind of weak momentum measurement as a measurement of the Poynting vector of classical optics.8

What the model assumes

  • Units with ħ = m = 1 inside the code; positions are shown in nm.
  • The coupling is impulsive and the pointer does not evolve between coupling and reading. Only the particle's transverse coordinate is coupled.
  • The joint distribution is tabulated on a grid of 360 values of yf across the screen (±400 nm) and enough values of Y to resolve σp. Runs are drawn from that table. A test checks the sampled strip averages against the exact ones.
  • Particles that hit the wall are not counted; the experiment starts behind the slits.

For instructors

The main point. Each run gives a pointer reading Y and a detected position yf. Averaging Y over the runs detected in one strip, and comparing with yf, gives a velocity. With a weak pointer and enough runs it is j/|ψ|², and the lines through the arrows are its flow lines. A sharp pointer gives different arrows, because it disturbs the wave.

The common mistake. Reading Y as the particle's position with a large error bar. Y is the pointer's coordinate. Only the average of Y over a post-selected set means anything, and the signal is a few nanometres against a 200 nm spread (the yf − Y button on the data plot magnifies it).

A sequence (about 25 minutes). Stage 1, five minutes: a few runs at 200 nm, then at 10 nm. Stage 2, ten minutes: before running, hide the predictions (Display tab: the two prediction curves) and ask students to predict the sign of vy at y = +300 nm; then run and compare. Stage 3, eight minutes plus about 40 s per map at 10 million runs per plane pair: map with 200 nm, then repeat with 10 nm.

Questions, with expected answers.

  1. Why does one reading with a 200 nm pointer tell little, and why is that useful? Its spread is comparable to the whole pattern; in exchange the coupling barely disturbs the wave, so the averages describe the undisturbed experiment.
  2. With the default settings (weak plane 500 nm, gap 50 nm), what is the sign of vy at y = +300 nm? Positive: the slope in the strip 300–320 nm is about +0.25, against an error of about ±0.05 at 1 million runs (only about 0.6% of runs land there). Link: ?stage=many&runs=1000000
  3. Find the pointer spread at which the measured arrows depart visibly from j/|ψ|². Below about 50 nm the green and dashed curves separate; at 10 nm they differ by more than the error bars over most of the screen. Link: ?stage=many&sw=10
  4. How many runs in one strip are needed for an error of 0.01 in the slope with a 200 nm pointer and a 50 nm gap? The error is about σp/(√N Δx) = 4/√N, so N ≈ 160 000 runs in the strip, about 3 million in total for a strip near the centre.
  5. The line ends sit at equal-probability positions of |ψ|². Which of the three views predicts this? All of them: it follows from the continuity equation for any flow that carries |ψ|². Link: ?stage=map

What is measured

The data are pairs of numbers: a pointer reading and a detected position. The velocity is an average over a post-selected set of runs, so it is not a property read off any single particle. The same data, and the same lines, follow from the Schrödinger equation alone. The views differ on what the lines are. Kocsis et al., who measured such lines for photons, call them “average trajectories”.3

Three readings of the same lines

Orthodox

All the data are in the joint distribution P(Y, yf): the scatter plot. An arrow is a regression of Y on yf, and the weak value is a statistic of that distribution. Quantum mechanics predicts it and it can be measured, but it is not a property of any single particle; in other set-ups weak values can even lie outside the range of possible results.2 Each run gives a weak reading and a strong result at one pair of planes. No particle is followed through the whole sequence of plane pairs: joining the arrows is the analyst's step, and the line summarizes an ensemble. j/|ψ|² is a property of the prepared state, not of any particle.

Pilot-wave

Each particle has a position and moves with velocity j/|ψ|². For a weak pointer the procedure returns that velocity at the strong plane, and the lines coincide with the guidance trajectories of the double-slit app.1,5 The pointer coupling also acts on the particle, so with a finite pointer and gap the agreement is only approximate, and with a sharp pointer it fails (try 10 nm). Within the theory the agreement is a consistency result, not a test of it. Turn on the flow-line overlay to compare.

Many-worlds

There is no particle beyond the wave, and the lines are not paths of anything. |ψ|² at a plane gives the weights the branches will have once a detector there records a position; the arrows summarize how that distribution changes from plane to plane, and their flow lines are a derived description. Each post-selected average is a conditional expectation relative to the branch in which the detector reports yf.

A limit of the procedure

The protocol returns j/|ψ|² whatever velocity law one assumes. Pilot-wave variants that add to the velocity a term which does not change |ψ|² predict the same data, so the experiment cannot single out the standard guidance law. Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì argue that within standard pilot-wave theory it is still a genuine measurement of the particle's velocity, and that it would not measure the velocity of such a variant.4

What the lines do not settle

Agreement between the measured lines and the flow lines of j/|ψ|² is predicted by all three views, so it does not decide between them. Nor does it say that the lines are where particles go in every arrangement: with which-way markers, Bohmian trajectories can disagree with what the marker records, the “surrealistic trajectories” of Englert et al.6 Mahler et al. reconstructed such trajectories with weak measurements and traced the disagreement to the nonlocality of the guidance law.7 Other readings of the same data do not involve trajectories at all. For light, Bliokh et al. read weak momentum measurements as measurements of the energy flow (Poynting vector) of classical optics.8 Vaidman uses weak traces, within standard quantum mechanics with pre- and post-selection, to say where a particle has been, without joining them into paths.9

Why it belongs here

The double-slit app draws the flow lines of j/|ψ|² from the theory. This app shows that the same field can be reconstructed from data that could be taken in a laboratory. Whether the lines are paths is what the three views dispute. It also shows the price: a weak pointer, post-selection and many runs per pair of planes, with only a fraction contributing to each arrow. The idea and the first version of these pictures are Anssi Kuha's.
