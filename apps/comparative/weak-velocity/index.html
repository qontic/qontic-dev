The setup

  1. Particles go through the double slit one at a time, as in the double-slit app.
  2. At the weak plane each particle is coupled to a separate pointer. The pointer moves by the particle's transverse position, but its own starting position is spread out, so one reading says almost nothing.
  3. A short gap later, at the strong plane, a detector records where the particle is.
  4. Each run gives two numbers: the pointer reading and the detected position. Nothing else is recorded.

The wave

The wave is that of the analytical double-slit app, with its default settings: wavelength 50 nm, Gaussian slits of width σ = 30 nm, 300 nm apart, screen 1000 nm behind the slits. Behind the slits the transverse wave is a sum of two complex Gaussians and evolves exactly in the paraxial approximation: the distance x from the slits plays the role of time, τ = x/vx, and the forward velocity vx = ħk/m is the same everywhere. The simulation uses the same coefficients as that app; a test checks that the two waves agree.

The weak measurement

At the weak plane the particle is coupled to a pointer, a separate system with coordinate Y. Before the coupling the pointer's wave is a Gaussian of spread σp centred on zero. The coupling shifts it by the particle's transverse position y:

ψ(y) φ(Y)  →  ψ(y) φ(Y − y).

Y is the pointer's coordinate, not the particle's. A single reading of Y has an uncertainty σp. With the default σp = 200 nm this is comparable to the whole interference pattern, so one reading tells almost nothing. In exchange the particle's wave is barely disturbed. A small σp gives a precise reading and a strong disturbance: try 10 nm.

The strong measurement and post-selection

After a gap Δx the particle's transverse position is measured precisely, giving yf. The joint probability of the two readings is computed exactly:

P(Y, yf) = |χY(yf, τ + Δτ)|²,   χY(y, τ) = ψ(y, τ) φ(Y − y),

where χY evolves freely for Δτ = Δx/vx. Each run samples one pair (Y, yf) from this distribution. Post-selection keeps the runs whose yf falls in a narrow strip (20 nm) and averages their pointer readings. Following Wiseman,1 the velocity at yf is defined as

vy(yf) = [ yf − E(Y | yf) ] / Δτ.

The simulation shows it as the direction vy/vx, the slope of the arrow. Its statistical error is the spread of Y in the strip divided by √N and by Δτ. Since σp is large and the displacement is a few nanometres, each arrow needs about a million runs.

What it converges to

As σp grows, E(Y | yf) tends to the real part of the weak value of the earlier position,2 and vy tends to

vy = Im(∂yψ / ψ) ħ/m = j / |ψ|²,

the probability current divided by the density at the strong plane. Here the particle moves freely between the two planes, so y(τ) = y(τ + Δτ) − p Δτ/m holds as an operator identity, and the limit is exact for any gap, not only for Δx → 0. A finite gap only sets the size of the signal. The bias that remains comes from a finite σp: the green curve in the velocity plot is the exact result for the chosen pointer, and the dashed curve is the weak limit. Below about 50 nm they separate visibly.

Stage 3: the field and the trajectories

Stage 3 repeats the experiment at twelve pairs of planes from the slits to the screen, each with fresh runs. The arrows give a measured velocity field. Starting points are placed at equal-probability positions in the first strong plane, and each line follows the measured arrows, interpolated linearly between planes and between strips. If the arrows are right, the lines end at equal-probability positions on the screen; the ticks on the right show where they end, beside the |ψ|² curve. This is the reconstruction done by Kocsis et al. with single photons,3 who weakly measured the photon's transverse momentum rather than its position, and post-selected on a strong position measurement in a series of planes.

What the model assumes

  • Units with ħ = m = 1 inside the code; positions are shown in nm.
  • The coupling is impulsive and the pointer does not evolve between coupling and reading. Only the particle's transverse coordinate is coupled.
  • The joint distribution is tabulated on a grid of 360 values of yf between −400 and 400 nm and enough values of Y to resolve σp. Runs are drawn from that table. A test checks the sampled strip averages against the exact ones.
  • Particles that hit the wall are not counted; the experiment starts behind the slits.

What is measured

The data are pairs of numbers: a pointer reading and a detected position. The velocity is an average over a post-selected set of runs, so it is not a property read off any single particle. The same data, and the same lines, follow from the Schrödinger equation alone. The views differ on what the lines are.

Three readings of the same lines

Orthodox

The arrows are weak values: averages over runs selected by a later result. Quantum mechanics predicts them, and they can be measured, but they do not say how any particle moved. Kocsis et al. call the lines “average trajectories” of the photons.3

Pilot-wave

Each particle has a position and moves with velocity j/|ψ|². In the weak limit the procedure returns exactly that velocity, and the lines are the particles' trajectories, the same as in the double-slit app.1,5 Turn on Pilot-wave trajectories to compare.

Many-worlds

There is no particle beyond the wave. j/|ψ|² is the velocity of the flow of |ψ|², and the lines are its flow lines. The post-selected averages are a property of the branches in which the detector reports yf.

A limit of the procedure

The protocol returns j/|ψ|² whatever velocity law one assumes. Pilot-wave variants that add to the velocity a term which does not change |ψ|² predict the same data, so the experiment cannot single out the standard guidance law. Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì argue that within standard pilot-wave theory it is still a genuine measurement of the particle's velocity, and that it would not measure the velocity of such a variant.4

Why it belongs here

The double-slit app draws pilot-wave trajectories from the theory. This app starts from data that could be taken in a laboratory and shows that they give the same lines. It also shows the price: a weak pointer, post-selection and about a million runs per arrow. The idea and the first version of these pictures are Anssi Kuha's.
