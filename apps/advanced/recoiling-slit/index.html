A wave model for the light and the atom

The light has two possible paths, A and B. Scattering correlates each path with a different atom wave. These are parts of one joint quantum state:

Ψ(s,u,t) = [fA(s,t)a(u,t) + eiφfB(s,t)b(u,t)] / √2.

Here s is distance along an optical arm and u = p/σₚ is the atom’s momentum in units of its initial standard deviation. The photon mode functions f describe equal Gaussian packets travelling along the two arms. The diagram enlarges their wavelength and slows their motion to make them visible. The ripples represent a one-photon mode amplitude, rather than a classical electric field.

The atom’s recoil waves

For recoil separation r = Δp/σₚ, our normalized atom waves are

a(u,τ) = (2π)−¼ exp[−(u + r/2)²/4] exp[−iτu²],
b(u,τ) = (2π)−¼ exp[−(u − r/2)²/4] exp[−iτu²].

The scattering is an ideal instantaneous momentum kick. After it, τ = σₚ²t/(2mℏ) gives free evolution of the atom’s momentum-wave phase. Both waves get the same free-evolution operator, so their overlap and the photon probabilities stay fixed during propagation. The animation uses a dimensionless delay τ from 0 to 0.6, independently of the enlarged optical travel time. It does not model evolution in the actual trap.

The left wave plot shows a and b, each normalized to one, as reference states associated with the two paths. Before scattering it shows the single initial atom wave. Select Real part, Imaginary part, or Probability density. The small phase rotation during the journey is calculated, rather than added as a decorative ripple.

Recombination adds amplitudes

The balanced beam splitter acts on the path labels. It produces two joint output amplitudes:

χ₊(u) = [a(u) + eiφb(u)]/2,
χ₋(u) = [a(u) − eiφb(u)]/2.

The right plot shows these complex waves as functions of atom momentum. Integrating their squared magnitudes gives the photon detector probabilities. With entanglement there is generally no single pure wavefunction for the photon alone; these output curves retain the atom coordinate.

P(D±) = ∫ |χ±(u)|² du = ½[1 ± V cos φ],
V = |⟨a|b⟩| = exp(−r²/8).

The output envelopes on the optical diagram have heights proportional to √P(D±). They show marginal probability envelopes; they are not a reconstructed pure photon field. When a and b overlap weakly, the cross term is small after integration. Reading the atom is unnecessary for this reduction in interference.

What detection changes in the display

Before detection, all three views use the same wave calculation. The two output amplitudes become amplitudes for distinct detector records. After detection, the Orthodox display keeps the selected χ and divides by √P(D) to normalize its conditional atom wave. Pilot Wave retains the two joint components and highlights one definite detector record. Many Worlds retains both record branches and displays their Born weights. The two many-worlds panels group branches by photon detector; a later recoil readout would create further record distinctions within them.

Photon counts sample a Born-weighted observer history in every view. The Pilot Wave record is sampled from the same distribution; this app does not derive it from field-configuration trajectories. Branch highlighting in Many Worlds is a display choice and never edits the counts.

The optional atom readout

Photon and atom readings come from the joint density |χ±(u)|². The atom data are sampled in correlation with the photon output, rather than independently. The wave plots refer to the state before this later atom momentum measurement; the marker shows the sampled reading. No exact path label is assigned to an individual event.

Limits of this model

This is a one-dimensional, pure-state Gaussian recoil model and a two-mode optical interferometer. Packet propagation is prescribed; we do not solve Maxwell’s equations or a complete atom–light Hamiltonian. The model omits trap evolution, heating, optical losses, background counts, and detector imperfections. The recoil slider is a ratio, rather than a calibrated trap-depth control. The record panels illustrate the three accounts of measurement and are not simulations of microscopic detector dynamics.

Orthodox

The photon–atom state contains both correlated alternatives before detection. The textbook collapse rule selects one recorded outcome. The selected detector’s atom wave is normalized; the unselected joint component is removed from the displayed post-measurement state. Entanglement can reduce the photon interference before this collapse.

Pilot wave

A definite physical configuration evolves under guidance from the quantum state. The state does not undergo fundamental collapse. The display retains both joint wave components while highlighting one definite detector record. Effective collapse in a complete model is associated with the actual configuration belonging to one macroscopic record branch.

Photons require a specified pilot-wave field or particle model; the massive-particle guidance equation cannot simply be reused. This app calculates quantum amplitudes and samples a definite record. It does not integrate an electromagnetic field configuration, solve its guidance dynamics, or draw a photon trajectory.

W. Struyve and H. Westman, “A minimalist pilot-wave model for quantum electrodynamics,” Proc. R. Soc. A 463, 3115–3129 (2007). This is an example of a field-based formulation, not a solver implemented here.

Many worlds

The quantum state continues without collapse. Detection correlates it with separate macroscopic records, D₊ and D₋. Both nonzero-weight record branches remain on screen. Within either branch, an observer records one outcome. The branch weights come from the squared wave amplitudes.

The colored recoil components before detection do not automatically represent separate classical worlds. Momentum separation alone is not a model of decoherence. Our two record panels stand for the later, effectively decohered detector records.

This experiment tests the shared quantum prediction. It does not select one of these three views.

What is the apparatus doing?

  1. Prepare. Hold a single atom in a laser trap and prepare its motion near the quantum ground state.
  2. Scatter. Collect light scattered in two opposite directions. Different photon directions are correlated with opposite recoil kicks of the atom.
  3. Recombine. Bring the optical paths together on a beam splitter. Their relative phase controls the detector probabilities.
  4. Detect. Count photons at the two outputs. Repeat while varying the phase to measure an interference fringe.

There are no literal slits in this realization. The atom plays the role of Einstein’s movable object. A detector click after recombination does not by itself identify the earlier optical path.

What changes the interference?

If the two recoil waves overlap strongly, the atom provides little path information and the fringes are strong. If they are easily distinguished, the fringes weaken. In the experiment the researchers changed the trap depth to change the atom’s momentum spread. Our slider varies the equivalent ratio of recoil separation to momentum spread.

Y.-C. Zhang et al., “Tunable Einstein-Bohr Recoiling-Slit Gedankenexperiment at the Quantum Limit,” Physical Review Letters 135, 230202 (2025).
